# Tibialis Anterior Tendinopathy — Rehab Protocol

**Case profile:** 49-year-old recreational athlete. Acute right anterior tibialis tendonitis / tendinopathy. Normally plays basketball and pickleball 3+ days/week, currently paused. Concurrent patellofemoral pain (PFPS) rehab on the same-side knee. Home equipment: Peloton bike, elliptical.

**This is consumer self-management content, not a diagnosis.** It assumes a clinician has ruled out the mimics listed in section 8, or that you will get it checked if the pattern in section 8 shows up. Nothing here replaces an in-person exam.

---

## 1. Summary

### What it is

The tibialis anterior (TA) runs down the front-outside of the shin, crosses the front of the ankle under the extensor retinaculum, and inserts on the medial cuneiform and the base of the first metatarsal [1, 12]. It does two jobs: **dorsiflexion** (lifting the foot — clearing the ground in the swing phase of walking, controlling the foot down after heel strike) and **inversion** (rolling the foot inward). Tendinopathy of this tendon usually shows up as pain and tenderness at the front of the ankle and the inner midfoot, sometimes with swelling along the tendon line, morning stiffness, pain with resisted dorsiflexion, and pain going uphill or upstairs [1, 12, 14, 29].

The AAFP review notes the classic presentation is anterior ankle and medial midfoot pain "most commonly at night, but also with activity," with a positive passive stretch test — passive plantarflexion combined with hindfoot eversion and midfoot pronation reproduces the pain [1].

"Tendonitis" is the common name, but in anything lasting more than a couple of weeks the tissue picture is degenerative/reactive rather than classically inflammatory, which is why "tendinopathy" is the preferred term and why the treatment is loading rather than rest [14].

### Why it happens

The consistent drivers in this tendon are:

- **A spike in load.** Training volume increases beyond roughly 10% per week, a return to sport after a layoff, a new court surface, a new shoe [12].
- **Uphill and stair work** — more concentric dorsiflexion demand to clear the foot [13, 14, 12].
- **Downhill and deceleration** — the TA works *eccentrically* to lower the forefoot after heel strike, and eccentric load is the highest-force mode for a tendon [13, 14, 15].
- **Direct compression from footwear.** Laces tightened hard over the front of the ankle press the tendon against the ankle bones under the extensor retinaculum. This is a mechanical, non-training cause and it is the single most under-appreciated one — skip-lacing around the sore spot alone can meaningfully reduce symptoms in early cases [12, 36, 16].
- **Foot mechanics** — overpronation or a stiff high-arched (cavus) foot both increase TA work [13, 12].

For a basketball/pickleball player specifically: repeated deceleration, split-steps, backpedaling, and lateral shuffles all load the anterior shin eccentrically, and high-top basketball shoes laced tight add the compression factor on top.

### Typical course

- Acute case driven by a shoe/lacing problem or a short training spike: **2–4 weeks** with lace modification and relative rest [16, 37].
- Case driven by sustained overtraining: **6–12 weeks** with a structured loading program [12, 37].
- Chronic (symptoms > 3 months): **3–6 months** of consistent loading [12, 37].
- Most people recover fully in **4–12 weeks** with proper care [37].

Your case is described as acute, so the realistic target is **8–12 weeks from today to unrestricted 3x/week play**, with meaningful symptom relief inside the first 2–3 weeks. That is the timeline this protocol is built around.

### Load-management principles (the actual mechanism of recovery)

These four principles are the protocol. The exercises are just a delivery vehicle.

**1. Tendons respond to load, not rest.** The JOSPT Achilles tendinopathy clinical practice guideline — the best-developed guideline in lower-limb tendinopathy and the standard source clinicians extrapolate from for other ankle tendons — recommends mechanical loading (either eccentric, or heavy-load slow-speed concentric/eccentric) to reduce pain and improve function, performed at least twice weekly within pain tolerance [2, 3]. Complete rest de-conditions the tendon and the calf/shin complex and sets up the same injury on return. Which loading flavor you pick matters less than that you do one: a head-to-head RCT found no difference in clinical effect between Alfredson-style eccentric-only loading and Silbernagel-style combined concentric-eccentric loading [39], and heavy-slow resistance matched eccentric training with better adherence and satisfaction [6]. This protocol uses the combined heavy-slow approach because it's easier to sustain.

**2. Total load = training load + daily-life load.** Walking is the biggest single TA loader in a normal day, and stairs and hills multiply it. Cutting sport to zero while still walking 12,000 steps a day with tight laces means you have not actually deloaded the tendon. Section 5 is not optional.

**3. The 24-hour rule.** This is Silbernagel's pain-monitoring model, validated in an RCT where athletes who continued loading under this rule did as well as those who rested [4, 35]. The rule:

> Pain **during and after** loading may go up to about **5/10**, provided it **settles back to baseline by the next morning** and does **not climb week over week**.

A more conservative and more commonly-used working version — and the one this protocol uses, given this is self-managed — is:

> Pain **≤3/10 during** the exercise, back to your baseline **within 24 hours**, and **morning stiffness no worse** than the previous day [5, 40].

The key metric is the *delta*: compare your baseline morning pain to your morning pain 24 hours after a session. If that delta is more than 2 points, the previous session was too much. Back off one step and rebuild.

**4. Progress by capacity, not by calendar.** Every phase below has objective exit criteria. Hitting week 4 does not put you in Phase C; passing the Phase B exit test does. The Cook & Purdam continuum model is the reason this matters — adding or removing load is the primary stimulus that moves a tendon forward or back along the pathology continuum, and a reactive tendon that gets overloaded again simply resets [9].

### A note on isometrics

Rio et al. showed 5 × 45-second isometric holds at ~70% MVIC produced immediate analgesia in patellar tendinopathy [5, 7]. That finding has been widely adopted, but honesty matters here: it was a 6-participant study, and follow-up work in Achilles tendinopathy, plantar fasciopathy, and lateral elbow tendinopathy found inconsistent responders and non-responders [7, 8]. Silbernagel herself has editorialized that the evidence for isometrics being *superior* to other contraction types is weak [7].

**Practical upshot:** isometrics are safe, easy, and low-load, so they are a reasonable Phase A entry point and a good pre-activity pain-reducer if they work for you. Test them. If a set of holds drops your pain, use them. If they do nothing after a week, don't waste time on them — move to Phase B loading, which is where the actual evidence for structural improvement lives [2, 6].

## 2. Phases

Frequency below is for the *tendon-loading* exercises specifically. Mobility, soft tissue, and the hip/knee PFPS work can be more frequent.

---

### Phase A — Calm it down

**Duration:** typically 7–14 days.

**Entry criteria**
- Acute anterior ankle / medial midfoot pain, tender to touch over the tendon.
- Pain with resisted dorsiflexion, or with uphill/stair walking.
- Baseline pain during normal walking > 3/10, or pain that flares for more than 24 hours after ordinary activity ("irritable" tendon) [5].

**Exit criteria (all must be true)**
- Pain **≤3/10 during a 15-minute flat walk** in modified footwear.
- Morning pain/stiffness **improving week over week** (lower on day 7 than day 1).
- **5 × 45s isometric dorsiflexion holds** completed at ≤3/10, with no flare above baseline the next morning.
- No swelling increase, no new numbness or weakness.

**Weekly frequency**
- Isometrics: **2x daily**, 7 days/week.
- Mobility, soft tissue, foot intrinsics: daily.
- Hip/knee PFPS work: daily (this stays running throughout — the knee rehab does not pause because the ankle hurts).
- Sport: **zero.** Cardio: elliptical only, see section 6.

---

### Phase B — Progressive loading (the main event)

**Duration:** typically 3–5 weeks. This is the longest phase and the one that actually changes the tendon.

**Entry criteria**
- Passed all Phase A exit criteria.

**Exit criteria (all must be true)**
- Pain **≤2/10 during a 30-minute continuous flat walk**, and no next-morning increase.
- **Heel walk for 45 seconds** at ≤2/10.
- **15 slow (3s up / 3s down) weighted seated tib raises at the same load on both legs**, right side ≤3/10 and not visibly weaker.
- **25 single-leg calf raises** on the right side (standard normative plantarflexor endurance value) [28] — this matters because a weak calf changes gait and dumps work onto the anterior compartment.
- Stairs up and down at normal pace at ≤2/10.
- Knee: eccentric step-down pain ≤2/10 (PFPS gate — do not enter plyometrics with a reactive knee).

**Weekly frequency**
- Heavy/slow tendon loading: **3x/week, at least 48 hours apart** — this is the heavy-slow-resistance dosing used by Beyer/Kongsgaard [6].
- Balance, mobility, foot intrinsics: daily.
- Hip/knee PFPS work: 3x/week strength + daily activation.
- Sport: still zero. Cardio: elliptical + Peloton (seated), see section 6.

---

### Phase C — Energy storage / plyometric prep

**Duration:** typically 2–3 weeks.

**Entry criteria**
- Passed all Phase B exit criteria.
- Able to jog 5 minutes on flat ground at ≤2/10 with no next-day flare.

**Exit criteria (all must be true)**
- **10-minute continuous flat jog** at ≤2/10, no flare at 24 hours.
- **3 × 20 double-leg pogo hops** and **3 × 15 single-leg pogo hops** on the right at ≤3/10, no flare at 24 hours.
- **Jump rope 4 × 45 seconds** at ≤2/10.
- **Lateral bound and stick, 6 each side**, landing controlled, no knee valgus collapse, ≤2/10 both ankle and knee.
- Hop-test symmetry: **single hop for distance and triple hop for distance both ≥90% limb symmetry index** (right ÷ left) [28, 27].
- 3–5 minutes of controlled decline walking at ≤2/10 (this is the specific eccentric TA stressor).

**Weekly frequency**
- Plyometric / energy-storage work: **2–3x/week, never on back-to-back days.**
- Heavy tib loading (maintenance): 2x/week.
- Hip/knee: 2–3x/week.
- Sport: still no games. Cardio: full range, see section 6.

---

### Phase D — Return to play

**Duration:** typically 3–4 weeks to unrestricted play, then indefinite maintenance.

**Entry criteria**
- Passed all Phase C exit criteria, including the ≥90% hop symmetry gate.
- Meets the full return-to-play checklist in section 7.

**Exit criteria (i.e., "you're done")**
- Two consecutive weeks of full-intensity play at your normal frequency (3+ days/week) with pain ≤2/10 during and no next-morning increase.
- No compensatory limp, no lace-loosening mid-game to get through it.

**Weekly frequency**
- Graded sport exposure per the schedule in section 7, **48 hours between court sessions** until week 5 of the return schedule.
- Maintenance loading: **2x/week forever.** Tendinopathy recurrence risk is high if loading stops [12].

## 4. Exercise details

### Phase A

**`ta-iso-dorsiflexion` — Isometric dorsiflexion hold**
Sit with the leg out, loop a band over the top of the foot anchored to something ahead of you (or simply push the top of your right foot up into the underside of a couch, a table leg, or your other foot), and hold a steady mid-range pull-up effort for 45 seconds. Five holds, ~2 minutes rest between, twice a day — the dosing Malliaras and Cook describe for tendon isometrics [5].
- **Cue:** "Steady pressure, not a squeeze — same effort at second 45 as at second 5."
- **Common mistakes:** Going to end-range dorsiflexion (find a mid-range angle that is comfortable — end-range often compresses the tendon under the retinaculum); ramping effort up and down; holding your breath; going so hard that pain exceeds 3/10.
- **Progression:** Increase effort toward ~70% of max, then move the ankle angle toward more dorsiflexion, then move to `ta-band-dorsiflexion`.
- **Regression:** Drop to 20–30 second holds at 30% effort, or push into your other foot instead of a band so you control the resistance perfectly.

**`ankle-pumps-pain-free` — Pain-free ankle pumps**
Seated or lying, slowly point and flex the foot through whatever range does not hurt. Purely circulatory and range-maintaining, not a strengthening exercise.
- **Cue:** "Move inside the quiet zone — stop before the sharp edge."
- **Common mistakes:** Forcing through the painful end range; going fast.
- **Progression:** Gradually reclaim range as pain allows.
- **Regression:** Smaller arc, fewer reps.

**`toe-tap-seated` — Seated toe taps**
Sit with heels on the floor and lift just the forefoot and toes off the ground, then set them down under control. Zero external load — this is the lowest rung of the dorsiflexion ladder.
- **Cue:** "Heels glued, toes to shins."
- **Common mistakes:** Lifting the heel too; bouncing.
- **Progression:** Slow the lowering to 3 seconds, then add a light ankle weight.
- **Regression:** Do one foot at a time, half range.

**`ta-self-massage` — Anterior shin self-massage**
Roll a ball or massage stick along the muscle belly of the tibialis anterior (the meaty part just lateral to the shin bone, upper two-thirds of the shin). Do **not** grind directly on the tender tendon at the front of the ankle.
- **Cue:** "Work the muscle belly, leave the tendon alone."
- **Common mistakes:** Aggressive cross-friction on the acutely painful tendon — this provokes it; some older sources recommend cross-friction massage for *chronic* cases [14], but it is the wrong tool in an acute reactive phase.
- **Progression:** Add more pressure as tolerance improves.
- **Regression:** Hands only, light pressure.

**`short-foot` — Short foot / arch activation**
Sit with the foot flat, and without curling the toes, draw the ball of the foot slightly toward the heel so the arch lifts a few millimeters. Hold 5 seconds. Foot intrinsic strengthening is specifically recommended in the AAFP foot/ankle tendinopathy rehab guidance [1].
- **Cue:** "Dome the arch, keep the toes long and relaxed."
- **Common mistakes:** Curling/clawing the toes; lifting the big toe.
- **Progression:** Do it standing, then in single-leg stance.
- **Regression:** Use your hand to passively shape the arch first so you feel the target position.

**`gastroc-stretch-wall` — Wall gastrocnemius stretch**
Hands on wall, right leg back, knee straight, heel down, lean in until you feel a stretch in the upper calf. Hold 30 seconds. A stiff calf means the shin muscle has to work harder to get the foot up, so this reduces TA demand.
- **Cue:** "Back heel nailed to the floor, hips forward, not down."
- **Common mistakes:** Letting the back heel rise; letting the arch collapse and the foot turn out.
- **Progression:** Foot on a slant board or step edge.
- **Regression:** Towel stretch seated, or reduce the lean.

**`soleus-stretch-bent` — Bent-knee soleus stretch**
Same setup, but bend the back knee slightly to shift the stretch to the lower calf/Achilles. Hold 30 seconds.
- **Cue:** "Bend the knee toward the wall, keep the heel down."
- **Common mistakes:** Bending so much that the heel lifts.
- **Progression:** Elevated forefoot on a book or step.
- **Regression:** Reduce depth.

**`quad-iso-wall-sit` — Wall sit (PFPS isometric)**
Back against a wall, slide down to roughly 30–60° of knee bend, hold 30–45 seconds. Mid-range knee angles are more comfortable in anterior knee pain than near-full extension or deep flexion [5]. Five holds.
- **Cue:** "Weight in the heels, knees tracking over the second toe."
- **Common mistakes:** Going too deep and provoking the kneecap; knees falling inward; letting the heels lift (which would load the TA — keep the whole foot down).
- **Progression:** Deeper angle, longer hold, or a ball squeezed between the knees.
- **Regression:** Shallower angle, shorter hold.

**`glute-bridge` — Double-leg glute bridge**
Lying on your back, knees bent, feet flat, drive through the heels and lift the hips until the body is a straight line from knee to shoulder. The JOSPT patellofemoral pain guideline puts combined hip- and knee-targeted exercise at the center of PFPS management, with hip work often preferred early [10].
- **Cue:** "Squeeze the glutes to lift, not the low back to arch."
- **Common mistakes:** Overarching the lumbar spine; pushing through the toes.
- **Progression:** `sl-glute-bridge`, then add load.
- **Regression:** Smaller range of hip lift.

**`side-lying-hip-abduction` — Side-lying hip abduction**
Lie on your left side, body in a straight line, lift the right leg toward the ceiling with the toes pointing forward (not up), lower slowly over 3 seconds. Targets the posterolateral hip, the specific target the PFPS guideline names [10].
- **Cue:** "Lead with the heel, keep the toes pointed straight ahead."
- **Common mistakes:** Rolling the pelvis backward; letting the toes turn up (which swaps glute med for hip flexors).
- **Progression:** Ankle weight, then a band.
- **Regression:** Bend the knee to shorten the lever.

**`clamshell` — Clamshell**
Side-lying, hips and knees bent ~45°, feet together, band above the knees; open the top knee without letting the pelvis roll back.
- **Cue:** "Pelvis stacked and still — only the knee moves."
- **Common mistakes:** Rocking the pelvis backward to fake range.
- **Progression:** Heavier band, or do it with hips lifted (side plank clamshell).
- **Regression:** No band.

### Phase B

**`ta-band-dorsiflexion` — Banded dorsiflexion (slow)**
Sit with the leg out, band anchored to something in front of you and looped over the top of the foot. From a plantarflexed (toes-pointed) start, pull the foot toward your shin over 3 seconds, hold 1 second at the top, and let the band pull the foot back down over 3 seconds. The slow lowering is the point — this is the heavy-slow-resistance style shown to match eccentric-only protocols in Achilles tendinopathy with better adherence [6, 2].
- **Cue:** "Fight the band all the way back down — the return is the rep."
- **Common mistakes:** Letting the band snap the foot back down; using a band so heavy the tempo breaks; moving the whole leg instead of just the ankle.
- **Progression:** Thicker band, then move to `seated-weighted-tib-raise` for a truly heavy load.
- **Regression:** Thinner band, reduce range, or drop to `toe-tap-seated`.

**`ta-band-inversion-df` — Banded dorsiflexion + inversion**
Same setup, but anchor the band to your outside (right side) so you pull the foot **up and inward** against it. This matches the TA's actual line of pull, and eccentric inversion work is what the tendon-specific sources recommend for this tendon [14, 13].
- **Cue:** "Big toe up and toward the opposite knee."
- **Common mistakes:** Rotating the whole leg at the hip; letting the arch collapse.
- **Progression:** Thicker band, slower eccentric.
- **Regression:** Reduce range, thinner band, or do pure dorsiflexion only.

**`seated-weighted-tib-raise` — Seated weighted toe raise**
Sit on the couch with heels on the floor and a dumbbell or kettlebell resting across the top of your forefoot (a towel underneath helps). Lift the forefoot up over 3 seconds, hold 1, lower over 3. This is your primary heavy loader and it is fully couch-compatible.
- **Cue:** "Drive the toes to the ceiling and lower like you're setting down glass."
- **Common mistakes:** Weight placed too far back near the ankle (kills the lever and the load); heel lifting; going fast when the weight gets hard.
- **Progression:** Heavier dumbbell. Follow the HSR loading curve — start at a weight you can lift ~15 times, and increase over 8–12 weeks toward a 6-rep-max load [6].
- **Regression:** Lighter weight, or back to `ta-band-dorsiflexion`.

**`wall-tib-raise` — Wall tibialis raise**
Stand with your back and heels against a wall and your feet about a foot-length out in front. Keeping the heels planted, lift the toes and forefoot as high as you can, then lower slowly. Bodyweight loaded, standing.
- **Cue:** "Heels pinned, pull the toes toward your kneecaps."
- **Common mistakes:** Walking the feet too far out (turns it into a hamstring stretch); leaning off the wall; dropping the toes fast.
- **Progression:** Feet further from the wall, more reps, add a band around the forefoot.
- **Regression:** Feet closer to the wall, or fewer reps.

**`heel-stand-forefoot-lower` — Heel stand with slow forefoot lower**
Stand on your heels with the forefoot lifted, then lower the forefoot to the floor over 4 seconds against gravity, using both legs to get up and mostly the right leg to lower. This is the eccentric-emphasis exercise; eccentric loading is recommended over concentric-only for foot and ankle tendinopathy [1].
- **Cue:** "Up with two, down with one, four full seconds."
- **Common mistakes:** Dropping the last part of the range; holding a wall so tightly you unload the exercise.
- **Progression:** Add a backpack; then do the lowering fully single-leg.
- **Regression:** Both legs on the lower; shorten to a 2-second lower.

**`heel-walk` — Heel walks**
Lift the toes and forefeet and walk forward on your heels with short, controlled steps for 30–45 seconds. Physios use this as a shin-loading and warm-up staple [32].
- **Cue:** "Short steps, toes high, no slapping."
- **Common mistakes:** Long strides; letting the forefoot slap down; doing it on hard tile with thin socks (uncomfortable and jarring).
- **Progression:** Longer duration, or carry light dumbbells.
- **Regression:** 15 seconds, or heel-standing in place instead of walking.

**`calf-raise-progression` — Calf raises, double → single leg**
Off the edge of a step, rise up onto the toes over 3 seconds and lower below level over 3 seconds. Start double-leg, move to single-leg on the right when you can do 15 clean double-leg reps.
- **Cue:** "Push through the big toe, don't roll to the outside edge."
- **Common mistakes:** Rolling onto the pinky-toe side; bouncing at the bottom; bending the knee to cheat the top.
- **Progression:** Single leg → backpack load → build toward 25 reps single-leg [28].
- **Regression:** Flat floor instead of a step; hold a rail for more support.

**`single-leg-balance` — Single-leg balance**
Stand on the right leg, arms relaxed, hold 30–45 seconds. Proprioceptive retraining is a standard component of tibialis anterior rehab [30].
- **Cue:** "Let the foot make small corrections — don't lock it rigid."
- **Common mistakes:** Gripping with the toes constantly; holding the breath; propping against furniture.
- **Progression:** Eyes closed → cushion/pillow → add head turns or a ball toss.
- **Regression:** Fingertip on a wall.

**`banded-lateral-walk` — Banded lateral walk**
Mini band just above the knees, quarter-squat position, step sideways 15 steps one way and 15 back, keeping constant band tension. Posterolateral hip work for the PFPS side of the program [10].
- **Cue:** "Stay low, keep the band tight even on the return step."
- **Common mistakes:** Standing up tall between steps; letting the trailing knee collapse inward; band around the ankles instead of above the knees (different exercise, less glute med).
- **Progression:** Heavier band, lower stance, band at the ankles as an addition.
- **Regression:** No band, or fewer steps.

**`sl-glute-bridge` — Single-leg glute bridge**
Bridge as before but with one foot on the floor and the other leg extended or knee hugged to the chest.
- **Cue:** "Level hips — imagine a glass of water on your pelvis."
- **Common mistakes:** Pelvis dropping on the free-leg side; pushing through the toes rather than the heel.
- **Progression:** Foot elevated on a step, or add a weight across the hips.
- **Regression:** Back to `glute-bridge`.

**`step-down-eccentric` — Eccentric step-down (PFPS)**
Stand on a 4–8 inch step on the right leg, and lower the left heel toward the floor over 4 seconds, tapping lightly, then return. This is knee-targeted work, which the PFPS guideline pairs with hip work rather than using alone [10].
- **Cue:** "Knee stays stacked over the middle of the foot the whole way down."
- **Common mistakes:** Knee caving inward; dropping fast; using a step so high the kneecap complains (if pain > 3/10, lower the step).
- **Progression:** Higher step, then hold dumbbells.
- **Regression:** Lower step, or shallow squat on both legs.

**`split-squat` — Split squat**
Feet in a long stance, right foot forward, lower straight down until the back knee approaches the floor, then drive up. Add a rear-foot-elevated variation later.
- **Cue:** "Straight down, not forward — torso tall."
- **Common mistakes:** Front knee travelling far past the toes (this shifts load onto the kneecap); stance too short; front heel lifting (which also loads the anterior shin).
- **Progression:** Rear foot elevated → dumbbells → deeper range.
- **Regression:** Shorter range, hold a doorframe for balance.

### Phase C

**`ta-fast-band-dorsiflexion` — Fast banded dorsiflexion**
Same as `ta-band-dorsiflexion` but with a lighter band and fast, snappy reps for 20–30 repetitions. This trains rate of force development in the muscle-tendon unit before you ask it to do it under bodyweight.
- **Cue:** "Quick and rhythmic, like a metronome."
- **Common mistakes:** Using a heavy band and losing the speed; letting range shrink to nothing.
- **Progression:** Slightly heavier band while keeping the speed; more reps.
- **Regression:** Back to slow tempo.

**`heavy-tib-raise` — Heavy weighted tib raise**
The Phase B seated tib raise, loaded heavy for 6–8 reps. If you have or buy a "tib bar," this is what it's for; a heavy dumbbell over the forefoot works fine.
- **Cue:** "Six hard reps, still 3 seconds down on the last one."
- **Common mistakes:** Load so heavy the tempo collapses; adding weight faster than the 24-hour rule allows.
- **Progression:** Add weight in small increments when 8 reps are clean and pain-free the next morning.
- **Regression:** Back to the 10–15 rep load.

**`sl-calf-raise-loaded` — Loaded single-leg calf raise**
Single-leg calf raise off a step edge holding a dumbbell or wearing a loaded backpack, 3 seconds up, 3 seconds down.
- **Cue:** "Full height at the top, full stretch at the bottom."
- **Common mistakes:** Partial range; bouncing out of the bottom.
- **Progression:** More weight.
- **Regression:** Bodyweight, or double-leg.

**`pogo-hop-double` — Double-leg pogo hops**
Small, fast, springy hops in place off the balls of the feet with the ankles kept stiff and the knees nearly straight. Energy-storage loading — the mode you have to reintroduce before returning to any jumping sport [5].
- **Cue:** "Bounce off the floor like it's hot — minimal ground contact time."
- **Common mistakes:** Squatting into every hop (that's a jump, not a pogo); landing heavy and flat; doing them on concrete when a wood floor or thin mat is available.
- **Progression:** More reps, then `pogo-hop-single`.
- **Regression:** Smaller amplitude, fewer reps, or hop with a hand on a countertop for partial unloading.

**`pogo-hop-single` — Single-leg pogo hops**
The same, on the right leg only.
- **Cue:** "Stay tall and quiet — you should barely hear the landings."
- **Common mistakes:** Drifting forward across the room; hip dropping on each landing.
- **Progression:** More reps, then hop forward/backward and side-to-side.
- **Regression:** Back to double-leg.

**`jump-rope` — Jump rope**
Continuous low-amplitude skipping, 30–45 seconds per set. Excellent bridge between pogos and court work, and it loads the anterior shin in exactly the rhythmic way basketball does.
- **Cue:** "Wrists turn the rope, ankles do the work."
- **Common mistakes:** Big high jumps; landing on the heels; going straight to 3-minute rounds.
- **Progression:** Longer sets, then single-leg or alternating-foot skipping.
- **Regression:** Shorter sets, or mime the movement without a rope.

**`lateral-bound` — Lateral bound and stick**
Push off the left leg, bound sideways, land on the right and hold the landing for a full 2 seconds under control before going back. Directly rehearses the pickleball/basketball side-step demand under supervision.
- **Cue:** "Stick it and freeze — if you wobble, you bounded too far."
- **Common mistakes:** Bounding for distance instead of for control; knee collapsing inward on landing (this is the PFPS-relevant fault); landing stiff-legged.
- **Progression:** Longer bound, then continuous bounding without the stick.
- **Regression:** Shorter bound, or step-and-stick rather than bound-and-stick.

**`deceleration-drill` — Run-to-stop deceleration**
Jog/run 10–15 yards, then stop over 2–3 steps and hold. Deceleration is where the tibialis anterior does its hardest eccentric work in court sport, so it gets rehearsed deliberately before games.
- **Cue:** "Sink the hips to stop — don't brake with the front foot."
- **Common mistakes:** Stopping on one hard stiff step; heel-jamming into the floor; doing this before the Phase C jog criterion is met.
- **Progression:** Faster approach speed, shorter stopping distance, add a change of direction after the stop.
- **Regression:** Walk-to-stop, then jog-to-stop over 5 steps.

**`decline-walk-loading` — Controlled decline walking**
Walk downhill on a gentle grade (treadmill at −3%, or a mild street hill) for 3–5 minutes at a slow pace with short steps. Downhill is the specific eccentric TA stressor named across the tendon-specific literature [13, 14, 15] — which means it is both the thing to avoid in Phase A and the thing to *deliberately reintroduce* in Phase C, because you cannot play a sport with deceleration in it if the tendon has never been re-exposed to eccentric load.
- **Cue:** "Short, quick steps — let the whole foot land, don't reach out with the heel."
- **Common mistakes:** Long over-striding heel-first steps; steep grades too early; doing 20 minutes on day one.
- **Progression:** Steeper grade, then longer duration, then normal walking speed.
- **Regression:** Flat walking only.

**`spanish-squat` — Spanish squat / loaded wall sit (PFPS)**
Loop a heavy band around the back of both knees, anchored behind you at knee height, lean back against the band and sit into a ~60° knee bend, holding 30–45 seconds. If you have no anchor, a weighted wall sit substitutes.
- **Cue:** "Shins vertical, let the band hold you back."
- **Common mistakes:** Letting the knees drift forward past the toes; band anchored too high or too low.
- **Progression:** Heavier band, longer holds, add a dumbbell held at the chest.
- **Regression:** Plain wall sit.

### Phase D

**`sport-stationary-skills` — Stationary shooting / dinking**
Free throws and set shots without chasing rebounds; pickleball dinking at the kitchen line without lateral movement. Skill exposure with essentially no deceleration load — the standard first rung in graded pickleball return progressions [31].
- **Cue:** "Feet stay home — this session is about touch, not movement."
- **Common mistakes:** "Just one game" creep; chasing loose balls.
- **Progression:** Add a few steps between shots, then add `shuffle-cut-drill`.
- **Regression:** Wall practice / shadow swings only.

**`shuffle-cut-drill` — Lateral shuffle + change of direction**
Cone drill: shuffle side to side, then plant and cut. Run it at 50% intensity in week 1, 80% in week 2, and full speed in week 3. Progression variables for court-sport return are area covered, rest interval, speed, and adding ball handling [27].
- **Cue:** "Plant on a wide base and push off — don't twist on a stuck foot."
- **Common mistakes:** Going to full intensity in the first session; crossing the feet; ignoring a knee that starts talking.
- **Progression:** Larger area, less rest, add a ball, add reactive (unplanned) cues.
- **Regression:** Slower, smaller area, longer rest.

**`jump-landing-drill` — Repeated jump with soft landing**
Countermovement jumps, focused on absorbing the landing through the hips and knees, landing quietly.
- **Cue:** "Land like you're trying not to wake someone downstairs."
- **Common mistakes:** Stiff-legged landings; knees caving in; landing on the heels.
- **Progression:** Single-leg landings, then landing off a low box, then jumping for a rebound.
- **Regression:** Back to `pogo-hop-double`.

**`scrimmage-graded` — Graded scrimmage / game play**
Follow the schedule in section 7. The rule that matters more than the schedule: 48 hours between court sessions until you have completed four weeks of graded return.
- **Cue:** "Set a timer before you start and honor it."
- **Common mistakes:** Playing "just one more game"; ignoring a 24-hour flare and playing again the next day.
- **Progression:** Per section 7 schedule.
- **Regression:** Drop back one week in the schedule after any flare.

**`maintenance-tib-load` — Maintenance heavy tib raise**
The Phase C heavy tib raise, kept at 2x/week indefinitely. Recurrence is common in this tendon if the biomechanical and capacity contributors are not addressed [36, 12].
- **Cue:** "Two sessions a week, forever — this is the insurance policy."
- **Common mistakes:** Stopping once it stops hurting.
- **Progression:** Keep adding load slowly over months.
- **Regression:** Drop to `ta-band-dorsiflexion` during a busy stretch, but do something.

**`maintenance-hip-knee` — Maintenance split squat + hip abduction**
Continued hip- and knee-targeted strength, which is the long-term backbone of the PFPS side of your program [10, 11].
- **Cue:** "The knee program doesn't end when the knee stops hurting."
- **Common mistakes:** Dropping the hip work first — it's the part with the best evidence in early PFPS management [10].
- **Progression:** Add load.
- **Regression:** Bodyweight only.

**`pre-game-iso` — Pre-game isometric dorsiflexion primer**
Three 30-second banded isometric dorsiflexion holds before warming up. Use it only if you were an isometric "responder" in Phase A — the analgesic effect is real for some people and absent for others [7, 8].
- **Cue:** "Test it once — if it doesn't help, drop it."
- **Common mistakes:** Doing them to full fatigue right before playing.
- **Progression:** Not needed; it's a primer.
- **Regression:** Skip it and just warm up longer.

## 5. Daily-life load management

Everything below matters more than the exercises in the first two weeks, because your legs do vastly more walking than rehab.

### Footwear — the highest-leverage change

- **Loosen your laces, and skip-lace over the sore spot.** Overtightening laces across the front of the ankle compresses the tendon under the extensor retinaculum, and this mechanical irritation is a common primary or contributing cause [12, 36, 16]. Skip the eyelet pair directly over the tender area entirely, or use a "window" lacing pattern to bridge over it. Clinical sources report lacing changes alone can reduce symptoms substantially in early cases [16].
- **Check every pair you actually wear:** basketball high-tops (worst offender — the collar and lacing sit directly over the tendon), work boots, hiking boots, ski boots, and even some running shoes with a stiff tongue.
- **Add a tongue pad or a folded washcloth under the tongue** to spread pressure away from the point of tenderness, if you can't get relief from lacing alone.
- **Shoe characteristics that help:** a firm, supportive upper (reduces the foot slap after heel strike that the TA has to control eccentrically), a moderate heel-to-toe drop of roughly 8–12 mm (less drop means more dorsiflexion demand), a padded/soft tongue, and good lateral stability [12].
- **Avoid zero-drop and minimalist shoes during the acute phase.** Lower drop means more dorsiflexion range and more TA work.
- **Over-the-counter arch support** is reasonable if you overpronate, since excessive pronation increases TA workload [13, 14, 1]. The AAFP review supports OTC orthotics for foot/ankle tendinopathy [1].
- **Don't go barefoot on hard floors all day** in Phase A. Slippers or a supportive house shoe beats a stone kitchen floor.

### Walking

Walking is the primary daily loader of this tendon: the TA fires to clear the foot in swing phase, and again eccentrically to lower the forefoot after heel strike.

- **Phase A:** cut your daily step count by roughly a third to a half, and hold it there for the week. Break walks into short bouts rather than one long one. Aim to stay under the 15-minute continuous threshold you're testing against.
- **Phase B:** rebuild by roughly 10% per week, guided by the 24-hour rule.
- Take **shorter steps** with a slightly quicker cadence. Overstriding with a hard heel strike maximizes the eccentric demand on this tendon [16].
- Avoid long walks on **uneven ground, sand, and trails** in Phases A–B — every irregularity is an extra dorsiflexion correction.

### Hills and stairs

- **Uphill and stair ascent** are concentric dorsiflexion demand [13, 12]. **Downhill and stair descent** are eccentric demand, which is higher force [13, 14, 15].
- **Phase A:** avoid hills entirely. Take stairs slowly, one at a time, and use a handrail to offload. Take the elevator when there's a choice.
- **Phase B:** reintroduce stairs at normal pace once flat walking is at ≤2/10; hills last.
- **Phase C:** deliberately reintroduce decline walking as an exercise (`decline-walk-loading`) rather than accidentally as a commute.

### What to avoid during the acute phase (Phase A)

- All basketball and pickleball. All running and jogging.
- Jumping, skipping, jump rope, plyometrics of any kind.
- Deep squats and lunges that force end-range dorsiflexion.
- Hill sprints, stair climber machines, incline treadmill walking, StairMaster.
- Aggressive stretching of the shin (kneeling on the tops of the feet) — a gentle version is fine later, but end-range positions can compress the tendon under the retinaculum when it's reactive.
- Cross-friction massage directly on the acutely painful tendon.
- **Corticosteroid injection into or around the tendon.** Chronic steroid exposure is a documented risk factor for anterior tibial tendon rupture [11]. If a clinician proposes an injection, ask specifically about rupture risk — this is a tendon that ruptures, and men over 45 are the highest-risk demographic [11].
- Kicking sports and anything involving forced plantarflexion against resistance [13].

### Ice, heat, and medication

- Cold for 10–15 minutes over the front of the ankle can help in the first few days for symptom relief [14, 30].
- Short-term acetaminophen or NSAIDs can help pain, but note the AAFP caveat that they may interfere with tissue remodeling — use them for a few days, not for weeks [1].
- If walking is genuinely painful, short-term immobilization (a walking boot or an ankle-foot orthosis) is a legitimate medical option and is the initial treatment named for this tendon specifically [1] — but that is a decision for a clinician, not a self-management step, and prolonged immobilization costs you capacity.

## 6. Cardio during rehab

The general principle: keep aerobic fitness through low-dorsiflexion-demand, non-impact modalities, and reintroduce impact only in Phase C.

| Modality | Phase A | Phase B | Phase C | Phase D |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Elliptical** | ✅ Best first choice. 10–20 min, low resistance, no incline, feet flat | ✅ Build to 30–45 min, add resistance | ✅ Full use | ✅ Full use |
| **Peloton (seated, low resistance, 85–95 rpm)** | ⚠️ Only with the foot-angle fix below; 15–20 min | ✅ 30–45 min seated | ✅ Full use including intervals | ✅ Full use |
| **Peloton standing climbs / out of saddle** | ❌ | ⚠️ Late Phase B, short bouts | ✅ | ✅ |
| **Walking as cardio** | ⚠️ Short flat bouts only | ✅ Flat, building 10%/wk | ✅ Including decline | ✅ |
| **Swimming (pull buoy, minimal kick)** | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ |
| **Swimming with hard flutter kick** | ⚠️ Sustained plantarflexion; stop if the front of the ankle aches | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ |
| **Rowing erg** | ❌ The catch position is deep dorsiflexion under load | ⚠️ Shorter slide, higher foot-stretcher setting | ✅ | ✅ |
| **Stair climber / StairMaster** | ❌ | ❌ | ⚠️ | ✅ |
| **Incline treadmill walking** | ❌ | ❌ | ⚠️ Low grade | ✅ |
| **Running / jogging** | ❌ | ❌ | ✅ Flat, starting at 5 min | ✅ |
| **Jump rope** | ❌ | ❌ | ✅ Programmed, see Phase C table | ✅ |

### Why the elliptical is the best Phase A choice

On an elliptical the foot stays in contact with a pedal through the entire stride, so there is no swing phase requiring the TA to clear the foot and no heel-strike requiring it to lower the forefoot eccentrically. The ankle stays relatively static. Impact and joint compression are also low, which is why it is a standard rehab cardio recommendation for knee problems as well [23]. Two additional advantages for your specific situation:

1. **The pedals are flat and unclipped**, so your foot can sit at whatever toe-out angle it naturally wants. This directly avoids the Peloton problem below. Some ellipticals also have pedals that pivot slightly to follow the foot's natural angle through the stride.
2. **Nothing is strapped across the top of your ankle.** If your elliptical has foot straps, leave them loose or off — a strap over the dorsum of the foot recreates the lace-compression problem.

Wear a shoe with a well-cushioned, loosely-laced upper on the elliptical too. The compression rule doesn't stop applying because you're indoors.

### Why cycling is low-risk for this tendon (and where it isn't)

Tibialis anterior activation during cycling is genuinely low. EMG comparisons of stationary equipment found TA activity around **19% MVIC on a recumbent bike and 30% MVIC on an upright bike** [23] — modest compared with the plantarflexors and the big hip/knee extensors. Timing-wise, the TA is active only during the **initial phase of the upstroke** and shows its largest burst around **top dead center**, the transition between upstroke and downstroke [24].

So: **does ankle dorsiflexion demand on the upstroke matter for tibialis anterior tendinopathy?** Mostly no. Seated cycling at moderate resistance and moderate-to-high cadence is one of the lowest TA-demand activities available to you, and it's appropriate from Phase A. The caveats are narrow but real:

- **Don't "pull up" deliberately.** Coaching cues about pulling through the upstroke increase the exact phase where the TA is active. Push down, let the other leg come around passively.
- **Clipping in and unclipping** is a forceful ankle rotation-plus-dorsiflexion action. If it's provocative, that's an argument for toe cages.
- **Toe cages with straps cinched down** put pressure directly over the tendon at the front of the ankle. If you use cages, keep the strap loose or leave it undone.
- **Very low cadence, high resistance grinding** increases ankle stabilization work generally. Higher cadence with less resistance is easier on the shin.
- Cycling shin pain does exist, and it's typically attributed to a fit issue (saddle too high or too low, cleat position) rather than the pedaling itself.

### Fixing the Peloton foot-angle problem

**The problem.** Your natural stance is somewhat toe-out (externally rotated). Most people have roughly 5–15° of natural toe-out [21, 22]. The Peloton's Delta-compatible pedals lock the shoe at whatever angle the cleat is bolted at, plus whatever float the cleat allows. Forcing a foot that naturally tracks at 10–15° of external rotation into a straight-ahead position loads the medial knee structures on every pedal stroke, and a cleat rotated even 2–3° off the natural angle creates repetitive torsional stress through the knee across thousands of strokes [21]. That is exactly the mechanism you're describing, and it's a well-recognized bike-fit failure mode. Cleat **rotation** is the axis that governs patellar tracking [21].

**Fix 1 — Use maximum-float cleats.**

LOOK's cleat color code: **red = 9° of float, grey = 4.5°, black = 0°** [19]. In the Delta family specifically, red (9°) and black (0°) are the standard options; the 4.5° grey is a Keo product, not Delta [19]. Aftermarket Delta-compatible cleats are sold in 0°, 6°, and 9° versions [42].

- **Confirm you are on 9° red Delta cleats, not 0° black.** If you're on black or 6° cleats, this is a $20 fix and should be step one.
- Understand what float does and doesn't do: float lets the heel swing **within a range centered on where the cleat is bolted**. It gives your foot freedom to move; it does not automatically center you at your natural angle. If your natural angle sits at the edge of the float range, you're fighting the stop on every stroke. So float and mounting angle are complementary fixes, not alternatives.

**Fix 2 — Rotate the cleat mounting to bias toward toe-out.**

The goal is to bolt the cleat so that your natural toe-out angle sits in the **middle** of the float range rather than at its limit.

The most reliable self-fit method — the one bike fitters use — is the self-selection method:
1. Loosen the three cleat bolts until the cleat can rotate under hand pressure but doesn't flop around freely.
2. Clip in and pedal easily with no resistance for 30–60 seconds, deliberately relaxing the leg and letting the foot find its own angle.
3. Stop, unclip carefully without twisting, and tighten the bolts without disturbing the position.
4. Ride 10 minutes. Fitters recommend making adjustments in small increments — 1–2 mm or a few degrees at a time — and keeping a 4 mm hex key handy for the first several rides [21, 22, 20].

If you'd rather set it directly: to move your foot toward **toe-out** (heel swinging toward the bike's centerline), rotate the cleat on the shoe so its nose points slightly toward the **inside** edge of the shoe. Change one thing at a time and ride before changing anything else.

**Fix 3 — Deal with the heel-strike problem that toe-out creates.**

Toe-out swings your heel *inward*, toward the crank arm. Past a certain angle your heel will hit the crank on every stroke, and that limit is what usually blocks people from setting enough toe-out. Two remedies:

- **Move the cleat toward the inside (medial) edge of the shoe.** Cleat lateral position governs Q-factor and hip-to-knee alignment [21]; moving the cleat inboard on the shoe pushes your foot outboard on the pedal, buying heel clearance.
- **Add pedal washers or spacers** between the pedal and the crank arm to widen the stance. A few millimeters per side is often enough.

**Fix 4 — Move the cleat rearward (fore-aft).**

Setting the cleat toward the forefoot promotes a quad-dominant pedal stroke with underactive glutes and hamstrings, which is associated with kneecap pain [20]. Moving the cleat **rearward** toward the midfoot shifts work toward the hips, reduces the ankle lever arm, and lowers both quad-dominance and ankle-stabilizer demand. That helps the knee and the shin at the same time. Move it back in 2–3 mm steps.

**Fix 5 — The nuclear option: get out of the cleats entirely.**

Given that the clipped-in constraint is what's causing the knee problem, and that you have an elliptical that has none of these issues, the simplest answer may be to stop using cleats on the Peloton.

- **Dual-function toe cages** bolt onto the existing Peloton pedals and give you a Delta cleat on one side and a toe cage on the other, so you can ride in sneakers without tools or a shoe change [25, 26]. These are widely available and cheap.
- **Replacement dual-sided pedals (SPD one side, toe cage the other)** install on a Peloton or any indoor bike with standard 9/16" threading using a 15 mm wrench [43].
- **In sneakers with a cage, your foot sits at whatever angle it likes** — the knee problem simply goes away. Keep the cage strap loose so it doesn't press on the front of your ankle.

Downside: without cleats you lose some pedaling efficiency and can't do hard out-of-saddle work as securely. For rehab cardio, that's not a meaningful loss.

**Recommended sequence for you:** confirm 9° red Delta cleats → self-select the rotation angle → add spacers or move the cleat medially if the heel hits the crank → move the cleat back a few mm → if the knee still complains after two rides, buy the toe cages and stop fighting it. Meanwhile, use the elliptical as your primary Phase A/B cardio, since it has neither the foot-angle problem nor any dorsiflexion demand.

## 7. Return-to-play criteria and graded return schedule

### Return-to-play checklist (all boxes before Phase D week 1)

This blends tendinopathy load-management criteria with standard lower-limb return-to-sport testing [5, 27, 28].

**Symptom criteria**
- [ ] Pain ≤2/10 during a 30-minute brisk walk, no next-morning increase.
- [ ] No morning stiffness at the front of the ankle for at least 7 consecutive days.
- [ ] No pain on direct palpation of the tendon, or only minimal (≤2/10).
- [ ] Pain settles within 24 hours after every rehab session for 2 consecutive weeks (i.e., a "stable" not "irritable" tendon) [5].

**Capacity criteria**
- [ ] 25 single-leg calf raises on the right, full range [28].
- [ ] 15 heavy slow tib raises at the same load, right and left, right ≤2/10.
- [ ] Heel walk 45 seconds, ≤2/10.
- [ ] Resisted dorsiflexion strength subjectively equal side to side, with **no weakness whatsoever** (any true dorsiflexion weakness is a red flag — see section 8).

**Function criteria**
- [ ] 10-minute continuous flat jog, ≤2/10, no 24-hour flare.
- [ ] 3 × 20 double-leg and 3 × 15 single-leg pogo hops, ≤3/10, no 24-hour flare.
- [ ] Single hop for distance ≥90% limb symmetry index [27, 28].
- [ ] Triple hop for distance ≥90% limb symmetry index [27, 28].
- [ ] Lateral bound and stick, 6 each side, clean landings, no knee valgus collapse.
- [ ] 5 minutes of shuffle/cut drill at 80% intensity, ≤3/10, no 24-hour flare.

**Knee (PFPS) criteria — you have to clear both problems**
- [ ] Eccentric step-down pain ≤2/10 at 8 inches.
- [ ] Single-leg squat to 60° without knee collapsing inward.
- [ ] No knee pain sitting for long periods or descending stairs.

**Practical criteria**
- [ ] You can play in your basketball shoes with laces at normal tension, with no tendon pain.
- [ ] Maintenance loading plan is in place (2x/week, indefinitely).

### Graded return schedule

Rules that override the schedule:
- **48 hours between court sessions** for the first 4 weeks.
- Apply the 24-hour rule after every session. Pain ≤3/10 during, baseline the next morning, no worsening week to week [4, 5].
- **Any flare → drop back one week** in the schedule and repeat it. Two flares at the same week → go back to Phase C loading for two weeks.
- Keep the maintenance strength work running through the whole return, on the days between court sessions.

| Week | Basketball | Pickleball | Volume / intensity | Sessions/wk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Stationary shooting, free throws, no rebounding chase | Dinking at the kitchen line, no lateral movement | 20 min | 2 |
| 2 | Shooting + light dribbling, layup lines at jog pace | Dinking + gentle controlled rallies, half-speed movement | 30 min | 2 |
| 3 | Shooting drills with movement, 1-on-0 cuts, half-court no-contact | Rec doubles, one game, 50–70% movement intensity | 30–40 min | 2 |
| 4 | 3-on-3 half court, controlled, no full-court sprinting | Doubles, 2 games, 70–80% | 40 min | 2 |
| 5 | Full-court scrimmage at ~50% of your normal game duration | Doubles at normal intensity, ~half your usual session | 45 min | 2–3 |
| 6 | Normal pickup game, one game only | Normal session, full intensity | Full session | 2–3 |
| 7 | Normal play | Normal play | Full | 3 |
| 8+ | Unrestricted, but avoid back-to-back full-intensity days for another month | Unrestricted, same caveat | Full | 3+ |

**Doubles before singles** in pickleball — singles covers far more court and demands far more deceleration.

**Pickleball is generally easier to return to than basketball** for this tendon: less jumping, less full-court deceleration, shorter sprints. If you want to get back to something first, make it pickleball doubles.

**Basketball-specific hazards for this tendon:** backpedaling on defense (sustained eccentric TA), hard jump-stops, defensive slides, and tightly-laced high-tops. Loosen the top eyelet or two on your basketball shoes for the first month back.

### Long term

Recurrence in this tendon is common if the biomechanical contributor is not fixed [36, 12]. Your permanent post-rehab habits should be:
- 2x/week maintenance tib and calf loading, indefinitely.
- Continued hip/knee strength for the PFPS side [10].
- Lacing discipline in every shoe.
- Never increase weekly court volume by more than ~10% [12].
- After any layoff longer than 3 weeks, ramp back over 3 weeks rather than jumping straight to 3 sessions.

## 8. Red flags — stop and see a professional

### Get seen urgently (same week, or same day where noted)

| Sign | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Weakness lifting the foot or toes, foot drop, or your foot slapping the floor when you walk** | Anterior tibial tendon rupture presents with weak dorsiflexion, and rupture is a real and under-diagnosed event in this tendon [11, 12]. Also raises peroneal nerve palsy. **Same day.** |
| **A palpable gap, dell, or depression along the tendon — or a new lump/mass at the front-inner ankle** | Classic rupture finding: a painless mass at the anteromedial ankle with dorsiflexion weakness [11, 12]. **Same day.** |
| **Pain that suddenly stops but weakness appears** | Rupture can relieve the pain while destroying function. This is the trap. **Same day.** |
| **Numbness or tingling on the top of the foot, especially in the web space between the first and second toes** | Deep peroneal nerve involvement / anterior tarsal tunnel syndrome, not tendinopathy [18]. |
| **Exercise-brought-on tightness, swelling, or numbness in the shin that resolves with rest** | Chronic exertional compartment syndrome — a different diagnosis needing compartment pressure testing [17, 33]. |
| **Increasingly localized, pinpoint tenderness on the shin bone itself that gets worse within each workout and worse from workout to workout** | Tibial stress fracture. The crescendo pattern and pinpoint bone tenderness distinguish it from soft-tissue shin pain [17, 33]. |
| **Inability to bear weight, or inability to bear weight persisting 1–2 weeks past onset** | Referral criterion in the AAFP foot/ankle tendinopathy review [1]. |
| **Hot, red, swollen ankle with fever or systemic illness; or crepitus (a creaking/crackling feel over the tendon) that is worsening** | Infection or aggressive tenosynovitis. |
| **Sudden onset during a forceful movement, with a pop** | Acute rupture; forced plantarflexion is the classic mechanism [11]. |

### Get seen soon (schedule an appointment)

- **No improvement at all after 2–3 weeks** of consistent load management and Phase A work. This is the most important self-management checkpoint in this document — an acute case that isn't budging in three weeks is either a different diagnosis or needs professional loading supervision.
- **Symptoms worsening despite reducing load.**
- **Night pain that is severe, unremitting, or wakes you repeatedly.** Nuance: mild night ache is actually a *typical* feature of this specific tendinopathy — the AAFP review describes pain "most commonly at night" as part of the ordinary presentation [1]. So night pain alone is not alarming here, unlike in most musculoskeletal conditions. But severe, escalating, or unremitting night pain, or night pain with fever, weight loss, or a history of cancer, always warrants evaluation.
- **Swelling that is increasing** rather than stable or improving.
- **Still not right at 8–12 weeks.** The AAFP guidance is that if pain and function haven't improved after 8–12 weeks, minimally invasive procedures become a consideration [1]. Other sources advise seeking a foot and ankle surgical opinion rather than continuing to wait if bracing and physio haven't settled things by 3–6 months [37].

### Differential diagnoses that mimic this

Worth knowing so you can describe your symptoms accurately:

- **Anterior tibial tendon rupture** — the big one, and the reason to take any dorsiflexion weakness seriously [11].
- **Extensor tendinopathy** (extensor hallucis longus / extensor digitorum longus) — very similar location, often lace-related, treated similarly.
- **Anterior tarsal tunnel syndrome** (deep peroneal nerve entrapment under the extensor retinaculum) — burning/numbness rather than mechanical pain [18].
- **Chronic exertional compartment syndrome** of the anterior compartment [17, 33].
- **Tibial stress fracture** [17, 33].
- **Medial tibial stress syndrome (shin splints)** — diffuse tenderness over a broad region of the tibia rather than focal, and pain typically present at the *start* of activity [17, 38].
- **Anterior ankle impingement** or an osteophyte at the front of the ankle joint.
- **Common peroneal nerve palsy** or an **L4/L5 radiculopathy** — both can cause dorsiflexion weakness and are named differentials for anterior tibial tendon problems [11].
- **Accessory bones or os naviculare** near the insertion — a reason weight-bearing X-rays get ordered [12].
- **Inflammatory arthropathy** (RA, gout) — also a risk factor for spontaneous rupture of this tendon [11].

### Personal risk factors worth flagging to a clinician

Men over 45 are specifically identified as a high-risk group for anterior tibial tendon rupture, along with people with inflammatory arthropathy, gout, diabetes, or chronic steroid exposure [11]. At 49 you are in that demographic. This does not mean anything is wrong — it means the "any dorsiflexion weakness gets checked immediately" rule applies to you with a bit more force than it would to a 25-year-old.
