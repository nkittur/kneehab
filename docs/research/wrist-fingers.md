# Wrist & Fingers — Two Upper-Extremity Programs

Target user: 49-year-old recreational basketball / pickleball player. Right wrist overuse
tendinopathy, plus a separate goal of making fingers harder to jam.

Not medical advice. This is self-management content built from published rehab literature.
Anything in Section 8 sends you to a clinician instead of to the exercise table.

---

## 1. Summary

### Program 1 — Right wrist tendinopathy (rehab)

Aching/sharp wrist pain that shows up **under load and at specific angles** (push-up position,
lifting, paddle contact) with no acute traumatic event is the classic presentation of an overuse
tendinopathy. The evidence-backed treatment across every tendon studied is the same shape:
**stop hunting for a passive cure and progressively load the tendon**, guided by pain response
rather than by a calendar. Rest alone deconditions the tendon and predicts recurrence — the
lateral-elbow rehab literature is explicit that patients should modify aggravating motions but
"not avoid all upper limb motions as this will further reduce the tendon's ability to take
load" [2].

The program runs four phases: **isometrics** (pain modulation + load without motion) →
**isotonic concentric/eccentric loading** (the actual remodeling stimulus) → **closed-chain
weight-bearing** (push-up position progressions, which is the specific thing that hurts) →
**sport-specific / return to play**. Progression is governed by two rules taken straight from
the tendinopathy literature: pain during exercise stays ≤3/10, and the **24-hour response**
(pain and morning stiffness the next day the same or better) is the gate to adding load
[1][3][4]. Expect **6–12 weeks** to be meaningfully better and up to **3–6 months** for a
stubborn case [1][7]. Because he plays a racket sport, equipment is a real load lever:
grip size and paddle weight change how much continuous forearm tension he generates per hour
of play [21][22][23].

Crucially, before loading there's a 5-minute self-assessment, because "wrist tendonitis" is at
least five different problems. Four of them (dorsal extensor, volar flexor, radial/De Quervain,
ECU) respond to the same general progressive-loading skeleton with variant-specific tweaks. The
fifth — **TFCC / ulnar-sided pain with forearm rotation and axial load** — is cartilage and
ligament, not tendon, and should be assessed rather than self-treated [11][12][13][14].

### Program 2 — Finger strength & jam-proofing (strength training, not rehab)

A "jammed finger" is a PIP joint sprain, usually a **volar plate** injury from the ball striking
the fingertip instead of landing in the palm [24][25][27]. Two honest statements up front:

1. **No study shows that finger strength training reduces jammed-finger incidence in
   basketball.** There is no RCT. Anyone claiming otherwise is selling something.
2. There *is* good evidence that the tissues involved **adapt to chronic loading**. The
   climbing literature is the best available proxy: elite sport climbers show thicker joint
   capsules, thicker collateral ligaments, thicker palmar plates, thicker pulleys and flexor
   tendons versus matched controls, and cortical bone thickness of the phalanges roughly
   **0.86–0.98 mm greater**, still increasing at 10-year follow-up [16][17][18]. That is direct
   evidence that the exact structures a jam injures are mechanoresponsive. Whether that
   translates to fewer or milder jams in a basketball gym is **extrapolation, and labeled as
   such throughout.**

So the program is built on three legs, ranked by how well each is supported:

| Leg | What it is | Evidence level |
|---|---|---|
| **Taping** | Buddy taping the ring/middle pair (or previously injured digit) during games | **Strongest.** Established treatment for volar plate injuries; mechanically blocks lateral and hyperextension stress, which is the injury mechanism [23][24][25]. Prophylactic use is standard practice, weakly studied. |
| **Catching technique** | Ball into the palm with soft, giving arms; meet the pass; don't reach one-handed with straight fingers | **Mechanistically strong, empirically thin.** The mechanism of injury is literally "ball hits the fingertip instead of the palm" [28], so technique changes address the cause directly. |
| **Strength / tissue conditioning** | Extensor band work, putty, grippers, rice bucket, dead hangs, fingertip loading | **Weakest, most speculative.** Grip training reliably increases grip strength (~10–30% over 4–12 weeks) [20][22], and connective tissue adapts [16][17], but the injury-prevention link is inferred. |

Structure is three levels — **Base / Intermediate / Advanced** — run 3–5×/week indefinitely as
ongoing training. Most of it is genuinely couch work: band, putty, gripper and rice-bucket work
all happen sitting down watching a game.

**One caveat the climbing data forces us to state:** the same climbers with the thicker,
tougher joints also went from 12.9% → 74.2% clear radiographic osteoarthritis in the DIP joints
over 10 years [16]. That's an extreme-loading population (repeated near-maximal crimping at
bodyweight), not what's being prescribed here — but it's a reason to keep finger loading
moderate and progressive rather than chasing maximal edge hangs at 49.

## 2. Wrist self-assessment

Run this once, take ~5 minutes, write down what you find. Do each test on the **left** wrist
first as a reference, then the right. The goal is to route to a variant, not to diagnose.

> **Rule for all tests:** "positive" means it clearly reproduces *your* pain, in a spot you can
> point to with one fingertip. Vague generalized discomfort is not a positive.

### Step 0 — Point to it

Point with one finger to the single worst spot. Then answer:
- Which **side** of the wrist? Back-of-hand (dorsal), palm-side (volar), thumb-side (radial),
  pinky-side (ulnar)?
- Does it hurt more when you **push** (weight-bearing, push-up) or when you **pull/grip**?
- Any **clicking, clunking, or snapping**? Any **swelling** you can see?

### Step 1 — Resisted tests (which tendon complains?)

| Test | How | Positive = | Routes to |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Resisted wrist extension** | Forearm on thigh palm-down, other hand pushes down on the back of your hand, you resist | Pain on the **back** of the wrist / into the forearm | Dorsal extensor tendinopathy (ECRB/ECRL/EDC). Also check the elbow — this overlaps with tennis elbow. |
| **Resisted wrist flexion** | Palm-up, other hand pulls fingers back, you resist | Pain on the **palm** side | Volar flexor tendinopathy (FCR / FCU) |
| **Resisted ulnar deviation + extension** | Forearm pronated, wrist extended, pull toward the pinky against resistance | Pain along the **pinky-side groove of the back of the wrist** | ECU tendinopathy [11] |
| **Resisted thumb extension/abduction** | Thumb "hitchhiker" position, resist the examiner pushing the thumb into the palm | Pain at the **thumb-side wrist**, ~1–2 cm above the styloid | De Quervain's (1st dorsal compartment) [6] |

### Step 2 — Finkelstein (radial / thumb-side)

The proper **Finkelstein**: grasp your own thumb and pull it into palmar flexion, then ulnar-deviate
the wrist. Sharp pain over the first dorsal compartment (thumb-side, just proximal to the wrist
crease) = positive [6][8]. The commonly-taught "make a fist over the thumb and bend toward the
pinky" version is actually **Eichhoff's** test; it produces significantly more false positives
and more discomfort than true Finkelstein, so if the fist version hurts but the isolated thumb
pull doesn't, downgrade your suspicion [7].

**If positive:** De Quervain's-type. Modify: skip radial-deviation loading early, add thumb
extension band work, consider a **removable semi-rigid thumb spica** for provocative activity
only — rigid full-time immobilization is not preferred [6]. Note that a thumb-side wrist that
hurts a bit *higher up the forearm* (4–8 cm proximal), with **crepitus / squeaking** and
sometimes visible swelling, is **intersection syndrome**, not De Quervain's — same conservative
approach, ~60% resolve in 2–3 weeks with activity change and splinting [9][10].

### Step 3 — Ulnar-side screen (the one that routes OUT of this program)

| Test | How | Positive = |
|---|---|---|
| **Ulnar grind / press-up** | Push yourself up out of a chair with palms flat on the armrests, then repeat with the wrist ulnar-deviated | Sharp deep pinky-side wrist pain |
| **Load + rotate** | Wrist ulnar-deviated, then rotate the forearm palm-up ↔ palm-down under axial pressure | Clicking, clunking, deep pain |
| **Doorknob / wringing test** | Wring out a towel, or turn a stiff doorknob | Focal ulnar pain, weakness |
| **ECU "ice-cream scoop"** | Forearm pronated, wrist extended + ulnar-deviated, make a scooping motion | Tendon **snapping/popping over the distal ulna** = ECU subluxation [11] |

**Interpretation:**
- Pain that is **superficial, along the tendon groove, worse with resisted ulnar deviation** →
  ECU tendinopathy. Treatable with this program plus a rotation-limiting brace for provocative
  activity; the literature also supports 4–6 weeks of splinting at ~30° extension + slight ulnar
  deviation for irritable cases [11].
- Pain that is **deep, focal, worse with axial load + forearm rotation**, with clicking or a
  feeling of instability → **suspect TFCC. Stop. Get assessed.** [12][13][14]
- **Snapping** on the scoop test → ECU subsheath injury. Also get assessed; it can coexist with
  a TFCC tear [11][12].
- Pinky-side pain **with pins and needles / numbness** → nerve involvement, not tendon. Get
  assessed [12]. A quick screen: press your palms together prayer-style with even pressure and
  shift toward the sore side — if that obviously reproduces the symptoms, think nerve, not
  tendon [15].

### Step 4 — Route

| Finding | Program variant |
|---|---|
| Dorsal, resisted extension positive | **Standard.** Full protocol as written. Bias Phase 2 toward wrist extension eccentrics. |
| Volar, resisted flexion positive | **Standard.** Bias Phase 2 toward flexion work; go slower on the push-up progression (extension end-range compresses the volar structures). Use fist/parallette variants earlier. |
| Radial, Finkelstein positive | **De Quervain variant.** Add thumb extension band; defer radial deviation loading to Phase 3; semi-rigid thumb spica for provocative tasks only. |
| Radial + crepitus, higher in forearm | **Intersection variant.** Same as above; expect faster resolution; taping over the area can eliminate the crepitus [9]. |
| Ulnar, resisted ulnar deviation positive, no click | **ECU variant.** Add ECU-specific loading; avoid end-range supination under load early; brace for play. |
| Ulnar, deep, rotation + axial load, clicking/instability | **STOP → Section 8.** Do not self-load. |
| Any numbness / tingling | **STOP → Section 8.** |

## 3. Phases and progression schemes

### Program 1 — Wrist, four phases

**Global pain rules (apply in every phase) [1][2][3][4]:**
- Pain during an exercise: **≤3/10** is acceptable and expected.
- Pain **24 hours later**: same or better than baseline, and morning stiffness the same or
  better. If it's worse, you added too much — drop back one step, not to zero.
- Progression trigger: no 24-hour flare and no more than a **+1 NPRS** increase for **2–3
  consecutive sessions** [2].
- Don't skip phases; do overlap them (Phase 1 isometrics stay in as a warm-up forever).

---

**Phase 1 — Calm & Isometric (typical 1–3 weeks)**

- **Entry:** anyone whose self-assessment routed into the program. Especially if the wrist hurts
  at rest, at night, or with light daily tasks.
- **Goal:** pain modulation, maintain range, load the tendon without moving the joint through
  the painful arc.
- **Dose:** isometric holds **~30–45 s × 5 reps**, roughly **40–70% of max effort**, **1–2×
  daily**. (Tendinopathy protocols use 5 × 45–60 s at 40–70% MVC, 2–3×/day; that's the ceiling,
  start lower [1][4].) Sub-maximal, boring, non-provocative.
- **Also:** full pain-free active range of motion 3×10 daily, unresisted [2]. Start the
  scapular/shoulder work now — the elbow/wrist literature explicitly pairs distal rehab with
  scapular strengthening as kinetic-chain support [2].
- **Modify:** cut provocative volume, don't cut everything. Reduce play, don't stop it, unless
  pain is >5/10 during play.
- **Exit:** full pain-free AROM; isometric holds at high effort with pain ≤2/10; no night pain;
  can do 3×10 unresisted wrist motion clean [2].

---

**Phase 2 — Isotonic loading (typical 3–8 weeks; the main event)**

- **Entry:** Phase 1 exit criteria met.
- **Goal:** the actual remodeling stimulus. Slow, heavy-ish, controlled concentric + eccentric.
- **Dose:** start **3 × 15** with light load (1–2 lb), progress load first, then toward
  **3 × 10 heavier**, eventually **3 × 6–8** at genuinely challenging load. Isometric →
  isotonic → eccentric-emphasis, advancing only without symptom exacerbation [1][2].
  **Every other day** for the heavy work (tendon needs ~48 h). Tempo matters more than weight:
  **3 s down, 1 s pause, 2 s up**.
- **Load reference:** the lateral-elbow program's Phase-2 exit is *20 reps of concentric/eccentric
  wrist extension with 2 lb, pain-free, no substitution* — that's a low bar and a useful
  checkpoint [2]. Phase 3 there is gated on grip strength ≥80% of age/sex normative values on a
  dynamometer, which is worth borrowing if you own or can borrow a Jamar-style grip meter [2].
- **Exit:** 3 × 15 with a load that is clearly challenging, all four directions (flex, extend,
  radial, ulnar) plus pronation/supination, pain ≤2/10 during, no 24-h flare. Grip strength
  within ~80–90% of the left hand.

---

**Phase 3 — Weight-bearing / closed chain (typical 2–6 weeks, overlaps Phase 2)**

- **Entry:** Phase 2 exit met **and** you can hold a 30 s hands-on-wall lean at ~45° with pain
  ≤2/10.
- **Goal:** this is the phase that fixes push-ups specifically. Wrist extension under axial
  compression is a different demand than open-chain curls, and the gymnastics/calisthenics
  rehab literature handles it by descending an angle ladder: **wall → high incline → low incline
  → floor**, plus quadruped weight-shifting and controlled loaded rocking [19][29].
- **Dose:** every other day. Ladder rung advances when you can do 3 × 10 at the current rung
  clean with ≤2/10 and no next-day flare.
- **Key trick:** a **fist / parallette / dumbbell-handle push-up keeps the wrist neutral** and
  removes ~60–80° of extension. If wrist extension is the limiter, use these to keep training
  the pushing pattern at full load while the extension tolerance catches up separately.
- **Exit:** 3 × 10 full floor push-ups on flat palms, pain ≤2/10, no 24-h flare. Can hold a 30 s
  loaded tabletop/plank on flat palms.

---

**Phase 4 — Sport-specific / return to play (ongoing)**

- **Entry:** Phase 3 exit met.
- **Goal:** re-introduce the sport's actual demands — impact, rotation under speed, repeated
  grip — in graded steps, and fix the equipment.
- **Dose:** graded return. Week 1: half-length session, 50% intensity, no competitive play.
  Week 2: full length, 75%. Week 3: normal play. Advance a step only if the 24-hour rule holds
  after each session. Keep 2×/week of Phase-2 loading forever — that's the maintenance dose that
  prevents recurrence.
- **Pickleball load management (do these regardless of variant):**
  - **Grip size.** Too small → you squeeze harder to stop the paddle twisting → forearm
    extensors under continuous tension all session. Grips around **4.125"+ circumference need
    less grip pressure**; too large restricts wrist mobility. Each **overgrip layer adds ~1/16"**,
    so it's a cheap, reversible experiment [21][22][23].
  - **Paddle weight.** Both ends are bad. Heavy paddles (>8.4 oz) add strain per swing;
    ultralight paddles feel unstable at contact and make you death-grip. The commonly cited
    comfort band is roughly **7.7–8.2 oz** for players with arm issues [21][23].
  - **Grip pressure.** Aim for a 4/10 grip, tightening only at contact. This is the single
    biggest lever and costs nothing.
  - **Technique.** "Arming" the ball — generating power from wrist/elbow instead of trunk and
    legs — overloads the forearm no matter what paddle you buy [23]. One lesson focused on
    backhand mechanics is a legitimate rehab intervention.
  - **Volume.** Cap consecutive playing days early on. Tendons respond to *change* in load, not
    absolute load; three 60-minute sessions on non-consecutive days beats two 3-hour marathons.
  - **Warm-up.** 3–5 min of light wrist/forearm range + isometrics before play [22].

### Program 2 — Fingers, three levels

This is **strength training, not rehab.** There's no injury to heal, no exit criteria in the
medical sense — just three tiers you graduate through and then maintain. Run **3–5 sessions per
week**; most sessions are 8–12 minutes on a couch.

| Level | Entry | Focus | Typical time |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Base** | Anyone. Also: anyone whose wrist is in Phase 1–2. | Extensor/intrinsic balance, tissue tolerance, tendon glides, submaximal grip. All low-load, all couch. | 4–6 weeks |
| **Intermediate** | Base exercises easy for 3 sets at the target reps; wrist in Phase 3+. | Introduce real resistance: firmer putty, a light gripper, rice bucket, pinch, wall fingertip loading, support-grip hangs. | 6–12 weeks |
| **Advanced** | Intermediate comfortable; wrist fully in Phase 4; no finger joint pain or swelling. | Higher-force crush/pinch, floor fingertip loading, edge/support hangs, reactive catching drills. **Optional** — the marginal jam-proofing benefit here is speculative and the joint-stress cost is real [16]. | Ongoing |

**Progression rule:** add resistance (next putty grade / next gripper / more weight) when you
hit the top of the rep range with clean form for two consecutive sessions. **Regress on any
joint pain, swelling, or morning finger stiffness** — those are the finger-specific analogues of
the tendon 24-hour rule. Finger flexor tendons and pulleys are slow-adapting; the climbing
literature's usual advice is that finger strength gains outpace connective-tissue tolerance, so
progress slower than you feel able to.

**Evidence honesty:** extensor band training, putty grades, and grippers all reliably increase
the strength they train (grippers ~10–30% crush grip over 4–12 weeks) but transfer poorly
between grip types — crush, support, pinch and extension are largely separate qualities and you
have to train each one you want [20][22]. Rice-bucket work has essentially no formal literature;
its plausible niche is **support-grip endurance and extensor work**, not maximal strength [30].

| `pinch-block-weighted` | Weighted pinch block hold | 4 | 15–20 s | hold | Pinch block + loading pin/dumbbell | 2×/week | gym |
| `edge-hang-20mm` | Half-crimp / open-hand edge hang, ≥20 mm | 4 | 7–10 s | hold | Hangboard 20 mm+ edge, feet assisted | 2×/week, 48 h apart | gym |
| `weighted-dead-hang` | Weighted support-grip hang | 3 | 15–20 s | hold | Bar + weight vest / dumbbell between feet | 2×/week | gym |
| `fingertip-pushup-floor` | Floor fingertip push-up (knees → full) | 3 | 6–10 | 3-1-2 | Bodyweight | 2×/week | floor |

## 5. Exercise details

### Wrist — Phase 1

**`wrist-ext-isometric`** — Rest the forearm palm-down on your thigh with the hand off the knee;
press the back of that hand up into your other hand and hold a steady sub-maximal effort. Nothing
should move.
- *Cue:* "Push into a wall that doesn't move" — steady pressure, normal breathing.
- *Mistakes:* Going maximal (should feel like ~5/10 effort); letting the wrist actually extend;
  holding your breath; picking an angle that hurts.
- *Progression:* Longer holds → higher effort % → do it at the mildly provocative angle.
- *Regression:* Shorter holds, lower effort, do it at the least painful wrist angle.

**`wrist-flex-isometric`** — Same setup, palm-up, press the palm up into the opposite hand.
- *Cue:* Forearm stays glued to the thigh.
- *Mistakes:* Recruiting the shoulder/elbow to help; gripping hard (keep fingers relaxed).
- *Progression / Regression:* As above.

**`wrist-ulnar-isometric` / `wrist-radial-isometric`** — Forearm supported thumb-up; press the
hand toward the pinky (ulnar) or thumb (radial) side against the opposite hand.
- *Cue:* Move nothing; the effort is all in the forearm.
- *Mistakes:* For a positive Finkelstein, the radial version can flare things — skip it early.
- *Progression:* Add effort, then progress into `radial-ulnar-hammer` in Phase 2.
- *Regression:* Reduce effort to 30%; drop the direction that hurts.

**`forearm-rotation-isometric`** — Elbow at 90° tucked to your side, make a loose fist, other
hand grips it and resists as you try to turn palm-up, then palm-down.
- *Cue:* Elbow stays pinned to the ribs.
- *Mistakes:* Letting the shoulder rotate instead of the forearm.
- *Progression:* → `forearm-rotation-hammer`.
- *Regression:* Lower effort; **for suspected ECU issues, skip end-range supination.**

**`wrist-arom-circuit`** — Move the wrist through flexion, extension, radial/ulnar deviation and
circles, staying strictly inside the pain-free range.
- *Cue:* Go to the edge of comfortable, not the edge of possible.
- *Mistakes:* Pushing into pain "to stretch it out."
- *Progression:* Bigger range as it opens up, then add load.
- *Regression:* Smaller arcs; use the other hand to assist.

**`tendon-glide-series`** — Cycle the hand through straight → hook fist → full fist → tabletop →
straight fist, pausing 2 s in each.
- *Cue:* Each shape is distinct — don't blur them together.
- *Mistakes:* Rushing; squeezing hard (this is a glide, not a strength drill).
- *Progression:* Add gentle overpressure; add `intrinsic-lumbrical-hold`.
- *Regression:* Do fewer shapes; assist with the other hand.

**`grip-submax-ball`** — Squeeze a soft ball at about half effort, hold 5 s, release.
- *Cue:* Half effort. If your forearm is shaking, back off.
- *Mistakes:* Max-effort squeezing while the wrist is irritable.
- *Progression:* → `gripper-light` → `gripper-moderate`.
- *Regression:* Squeeze a rolled towel instead; drop the hold to 2 s.

**`band-scap-row`** — Anchor a band at chest height, sit tall, row with elbows close and hold the
squeeze 5 s. Shoulder/scapular strength is part of forearm rehab, not a side dish [2].
- *Cue:* "Back pocket with your elbow," chin tucked, no shrug.
- *Mistakes:* Shrugging; yanking; letting the wrist do the work — hold the handle in a neutral,
  relaxed grip.
- *Progression:* Heavier band, single-arm, longer lever.
- *Regression:* Lighter band; do it as a pure scapular squeeze with no band at all [2].

**`wrist-ext-stretch`** — Arm straight, palm down, gently pull the fingers toward you.
- *Cue:* Mild pull, never sharp.
- *Mistakes:* Cranking on it. If it's >3/10, stop — the lateral elbow literature modifies this
  stretch when the Mill's-position pain is above 3/10 [2].
- *Progression:* Straighten the elbow more; add finger extension.
- *Regression:* Bend the elbow; drop to 15 s.

### Wrist — Phase 2

**`wrist-ext-curl`** — Forearm resting palm-down on your thigh, hand past the knee, holding a
light dumbbell; curl the back of the hand up, then lower for a 3-count.
- *Cue:* The forearm never leaves the thigh — only the wrist moves.
- *Mistakes:* Too much weight (2 lb is a legitimate starting load [2]); bouncing at the bottom;
  lifting the forearm to cheat.
- *Progression:* +1 lb when 3×15 is clean two sessions running; then shift toward 3×8 heavier.
- *Regression:* No weight; then `wrist-ext-isometric`.

**`wrist-flex-curl`** — Same but palm-up.
- *Cue:* Let the weight roll to the fingertips at the bottom, then curl it back up.
- *Mistakes:* Overloading — the flexors are ~3× stronger than the extensors, so it's tempting to
  jump weight here and then be sore everywhere [26].
- *Progression / Regression:* As above.

**`wrist-ext-eccentric`** — Lift the dumbbell into wrist extension using your *other* hand, then
lower it with the sore wrist over a slow 4-count.
- *Cue:* "Fight it down for four seconds."
- *Mistakes:* Dropping it; using the same weight as the concentric version (eccentrics tolerate
  more, but ramp gradually).
- *Progression:* More weight; then remove the assist and make it a full concentric/eccentric.
- *Regression:* Fewer reps; 2-second lowering; less weight.

**`radial-ulnar-hammer`** — Forearm on thigh with the thumb up, holding a hammer near the end of
the handle; tilt the head up and down under control.
- *Cue:* Choke up on the handle to make it easier, down to make it harder — a free load dial.
- *Mistakes:* Rotating the forearm instead of deviating the wrist; too long a lever too early.
- *Progression:* Slide the hand further from the head.
- *Regression:* Choke way up; drop the radial half if Finkelstein was positive.

**`forearm-rotation-hammer`** — Elbow pinned at 90°, hammer held vertically, rotate palm-up to
palm-down slowly.
- *Cue:* Elbow stays against the ribs the whole set.
- *Mistakes:* Swinging from the shoulder. **If ECU is the variant, stop short of full supination
  under load early on** [11].
- *Progression:* Longer lever.
- *Regression:* Choke up; use no weight.

**`flexbar-tyler-twist`** — Hold the bar vertically in the sore hand with the wrist extended,
twist it with the other hand, then extend both arms and slowly let the sore wrist unwind over 4
seconds. This is the best-supported eccentric loading tool for extensor-side pain [2][26].
- *Cue:* The good hand does the work; the sore wrist only resists the unwind.
- *Mistakes:* Too stiff a bar (start green or red, not blue); unwinding fast.
- *Progression:* Stiffer bar; slower unwind.
- *Regression:* Lower-resistance bar; fewer reps.

**`thumb-ext-band`** — Loop a small band around the thumb and index finger and spread the thumb
away from the palm against the band, then return slowly. Primary add-on for the De Quervain
variant.
- *Cue:* Move at the base of the thumb, not the tip.
- *Mistakes:* Band too stiff early — this compartment is easily irritated.
- *Progression:* Firmer band; add radial deviation once symptoms settle.
- *Regression:* No band, active motion only.

**`ecu-ulnar-extension`** — Forearm pronated on the thigh, light dumbbell, extend + ulnar-deviate
in one diagonal motion ("pull toward the pinky corner"), lower slowly. The ECU-specific loading
step [11].
- *Cue:* Draw a diagonal to the pinky-side corner.
- *Mistakes:* Pure extension with no ulnar deviation; too much weight (start at 1 lb).
- *Progression:* More weight; then add rotation under load; then weight-bearing.
- *Regression:* Isometric only; brace during provocative activity.

**`finger-ext-band`** — Rubber band around all five fingertips, open the hand against it, close
slowly. Doubles as the base of the finger program; a wrist in Phase 2 can already do this.
- *Cue:* Spread wide, then resist the closing.
- *Mistakes:* Only the index and thumb doing the work; snapping shut.
- *Progression:* Two bands, then a graded extensor device.
- *Regression:* Thinner band; a hair tie; loop it lower on the fingers.

**`wrist-roller`** — Roll a weighted cord up onto a bar/roller with alternating wrist extension,
then lower under control.
- *Cue:* Elbows locked at shoulder height, wrists do everything.
- *Mistakes:* Loading heavy immediately — the eccentric lowering is where forearms get wrecked.
- *Progression:* More weight; more up/down cycles.
- *Regression:* 2.5 lb; do only the upward (extension) direction.

**`farmer-carry-light`** — Walk with a heavy weight in each hand, shoulders down, wrists neutral.
- *Cue:* Stand tall, don't let the weights pull you into a shrug.
- *Mistakes:* Death-gripping; letting the wrist flop into flexion.
- *Progression:* Heavier, longer, or single-arm (adds a core demand).
- *Regression:* Lighter; a static hold instead of a walk.

### Wrist — Phase 3

**`wall-lean-hold`** — Hands flat on a wall at shoulder height, lean in so real weight goes
through the wrists, hold.
- *Cue:* Push the floor away — actively press, don't sag.
- *Mistakes:* Standing too close (no load) or so far it's painful.
- *Progression:* Step feet back to increase angle; then `wall-pushup`.
- *Regression:* Step closer; put the hands higher.

**`wall-pushup`** — Standard push-up against a wall, slow.
- *Cue:* Ribs down, body one line, elbows at ~45°.
- *Mistakes:* Bouncing; letting the hips lead.
- *Progression:* Feet further back → counter → couch seat → floor.
- *Regression:* Back to the isometric hold.

**`incline-pushup-high` / `incline-pushup-low`** — Push-ups with hands on a counter, then a
couch seat or stair. This is the angle ladder that gymnastics wrist rehab uses to restore
weight-bearing tolerance [19][29].
- *Cue:* Grip the surface with the fingertips to spread load away from the heel of the hand.
- *Mistakes:* Skipping rungs; sagging hips; rushing the descent.
- *Progression:* Lower surface; add reps; slow the eccentric.
- *Regression:* Higher surface; fewer reps.

**`quadruped-wrist-rock`** — On hands and knees, palms flat, rock slowly forward and back so the
wrist moves through extension under partial body weight.
- *Cue:* Rock only as far forward as stays ≤2/10.
- *Mistakes:* Rocking into pain; locked elbows; holding the breath.
- *Progression:* Rock further; shift weight to one arm; go to a plank base.
- *Regression:* Fists instead of palms; forearms down.

**`quadruped-shoulder-tap`** — In a quadruped or knee-plank position, tap the opposite shoulder,
alternating — adds a single-arm weight-bearing demand with a rotation component [15].
- *Cue:* Hips stay square — don't twist to reach.
- *Mistakes:* Fast taps; hips rolling.
- *Progression:* Knees off the floor; feet wider → narrower.
- *Regression:* Knuckles/fists; do it against a wall standing.

**`fist-pushup`** — Push-up on fists (or parallettes / dumbbell handles) to keep the wrist
neutral. This is how you keep training pushing strength at full load while extension tolerance
is still the limiter.
- *Cue:* Fists point forward, wrists locked straight in line with the forearm.
- *Mistakes:* Fists collapsing into flexion; doing them on a hard floor without a mat.
- *Progression:* → `full-pushup` on flat palms.
- *Regression:* Incline fist push-up.

**`full-pushup`** — Flat palms on the floor, full range, 3-second lowering.
- *Cue:* Screw the hands outward into the floor and grip with the fingers.
- *Mistakes:* All the weight on the heel of the hand; flaring elbows to 90°.
- *Progression:* Reps, tempo, then feet elevated; eventually `plyo-pushup-optional`.
- *Regression:* Back to fists or an incline.

**`plank-hold-palms`** — Plank on flat palms, actively pressing.
- *Cue:* Push the floor away, ribs down, glutes on.
- *Mistakes:* Sagging into the wrists passively.
- *Progression:* Longer holds; add shoulder taps.
- *Regression:* Forearm plank (zero wrist load) or fists.

**`dead-hang-wrist-check`** — Passive hang from a bar. This is a *test* in Phase 3: hanging loads
the wrist in traction rather than compression, so it usually clears earlier than push-ups.
- *Cue:* Shoulders active (slight pull down), not fully dumped.
- *Mistakes:* Doing it while the wrist is still acute; jumping off carelessly.
- *Progression:* → `dead-hang-support` in the finger program.
- *Regression:* Feet on the ground taking part of the weight; use a rack pull instead.

### Wrist — Phase 4

**`paddle-shadow-swings`** — Shadow the full pickleball stroke set (dinks, drives, backhands)
with no ball, building from slow to full speed.
- *Cue:* Loose grip — target roughly 4/10 grip pressure — and rotate from the trunk, not the
  wrist [23].
- *Mistakes:* Rehearsing the same over-gripping "arming" pattern that caused the problem.
- *Progression:* Full speed → live ball → competitive play.
- *Regression:* Slower, fewer reps, shorter swings.

**`dribble-control-drill`** — Stationary dribbling series, both hands, varying height.
- *Cue:* Push the ball with the fingerpads, wrist relaxed.
- *Mistakes:* Slapping the ball with a stiff wrist.
- *Progression:* Faster, lower, two balls.
- *Regression:* Slower and higher; fewer sets.

**`wall-ball-toss`** — Two-hand chest toss into a wall, catch, repeat.
- *Cue:* Catch soft — absorb by letting the elbows bend.
- *Mistakes:* Catching with straight, rigid arms.
- *Progression:* Heavier ball, closer wall, faster tempo.
- *Regression:* Lighter ball, further back.

**`med-ball-chest-pass`** — Explosive chest pass into a wall. The ECU rehab literature uses
medicine-ball throws at varied wrist angles as a late-stage loading step [11].
- *Cue:* Full arm extension, finish with the wrists neutral and firm.
- *Mistakes:* Too heavy a ball too early; letting the wrist hyperextend on the catch.
- *Progression:* Heavier ball; add overhead and rotational passes.
- *Regression:* Lighter ball; two-hand chest pass only, no catch (let it drop).

**`plyo-pushup-optional`** — Hand-release or clapping push-up. Genuinely optional; only useful if
he wants full impact tolerance.
- *Cue:* Land with soft elbows and fingers gripping.
- *Mistakes:* Doing these before flat-palm push-ups are painless.
- *Progression:* Higher, faster.
- *Regression:* Hand-release push-up (lift the hands, no jump).

**`graded-return-schedule`** — Three weeks: 50% → 75% → 100% duration and intensity, advancing
only if the 24-hour rule holds each time.
- *Cue:* Judge the previous session by tomorrow morning, not by how it felt at the time.
- *Mistakes:* Feeling good in week one and jumping straight to a tournament.
- *Progression:* Add competitive play last.
- *Regression:* Repeat the previous week's dose.

**`wrist-maintenance-block`** — Two sets of Phase-2 loading, twice a week, permanently. This is
the recurrence-prevention dose.
- *Cue:* Non-negotiable, ten minutes, on the couch.
- *Mistakes:* Stopping the moment it stops hurting — the most common cause of recurrence.

### Fingers — Level 1 (Base)

**`finger-ext-band-base`** — Band around all fingertips (and thumb), open the hand fully, close
slowly. Trains the extensors, which are chronically outmatched by the flexors and are the
neglected half of hand strength [26].
- *Cue:* Spread as if pushing four walls apart, then take 2 seconds to close.
- *Mistakes:* Index and thumb doing all the work; band placed too far down toward the knuckles.
- *Progression:* Two bands; a graded extensor trainer; then `finger-ext-eccentric`.
- *Regression:* Thinner band; band around three fingers only.

**`putty-gross-grip`** — Squeeze a ball of therapy putty in a full-hand grip, roll it out, repeat.
Putty is color-graded so resistance is dosable one step at a time [31].
- *Cue:* Squeeze until the putty is fully through the fingers, not just dented.
- *Mistakes:* Skipping grades too fast — the single most common error in home hand programs [31].
- *Progression:* Next putty color when the current one is easy for 3 sets.
- *Regression:* Softer grade; fewer reps.

**`putty-tip-pinch`** — Pinch a piece of putty between the thumb tip and each finger tip in turn.
- *Cue:* Pinch tip-to-tip, keeping the finger joints slightly bent, not collapsed.
- *Mistakes:* Letting the thumb IP joint hyperextend; only using the index.
- *Progression:* Firmer putty; add a 3-second hold.
- *Regression:* Softer putty; index and middle only.

**`putty-finger-spread`** — Wrap putty around two adjacent fingers and spread them apart against
it; work through all the pairs.
- *Cue:* Move at the knuckles; keep the fingers straight.
- *Mistakes:* Using the wrist to help; skipping the ring/little pair (the weakest and the pair
  most often buddy-taped).
- *Progression:* Firmer putty; hold the spread 3 s.
- *Regression:* Softer putty; use a rubber band around two fingers.

**`tendon-glide-fingers`** — Same glide sequence as the wrist program: straight → hook → full
fist → tabletop → straight fist.
- *Cue:* Hit each distinct shape and pause.
- *Mistakes:* Blurring the shapes; going fast.
- *Progression:* Add gentle overpressure into each end position.
- *Regression:* Fewer positions.

**`intrinsic-lumbrical-hold`** — Hold the "tabletop" hand shape (knuckles bent ~90°, fingers
straight) and resist gently with the other hand or a band across the fingers.
- *Cue:* Knuckles bent, finger joints dead straight — a right angle at the knuckle only.
- *Mistakes:* Curling the fingertips (that's the flexors taking over).
- *Progression:* Add band resistance; hold longer.
- *Regression:* No resistance; shorter holds.

**`finger-lifts-table`** — Palm flat on a table, lift one finger at a time as high as possible
and hold 2 s.
- *Cue:* Keep the other four fingers pressed flat.
- *Mistakes:* Lifting the whole hand; rushing.
- *Progression:* Add a light weight or band over the finger; lift two non-adjacent fingers.
- *Regression:* Assist the lift with the other hand.

**`thumb-opposition-putty`** — Press the thumb pad to each fingertip with putty between,
squeezing to a flat disc.
- *Cue:* Form a round "O," not a pinched flat shape.
- *Mistakes:* Collapsing the thumb joint.
- *Progression:* Firmer putty.
- *Regression:* Softer putty; index and middle only.

**`gripper-light`** — An easy-setting adjustable gripper for high reps. Grippers train **crush
grip** specifically, which doesn't fully transfer to support or pinch grip — so this is one of
three grip qualities, not all of them [20][22].
- *Cue:* Full close, full open, controlled both directions.
- *Mistakes:* Partial reps; going heavy immediately; doing this while the wrist is acute
  (see Section 7).
- *Progression:* Increase the setting when 3×20 is easy → `gripper-moderate`.
- *Regression:* Easier setting; squeeze a ball instead.

**`catch-technique-drill`** — Deliberate slow-motion catches focusing on the palm as the target.
Since the injury mechanism is "the ball hits the fingertip instead of the palm" [28], this is the
most directly causal thing in the whole program.
- *Cue:* **"Meet the ball, thumbs in, ball into the palms, then give."** Two hands whenever
  possible; move toward the pass rather than reaching for it.
- *Mistakes:* Catching with stiff, straight, splayed fingers; one-handed stabs at chest passes;
  ball arriving at the fingertips because you reached late.
- *Progression:* Faster passes → off-angle passes → `buddy-tape-catch-drill` → `reactive-catch-wall`.
- *Regression:* Slower, softer ball, closer range.

### Fingers — Level 2 (Intermediate)

**`finger-ext-band-heavy`** — Same as the base version with a firm band or a purpose-built
extensor trainer, at 3 sets of 12–20 with progressive resistance [26].
- *Cue:* Full spread; keep the wrist neutral, not extended.
- *Mistakes:* Letting the wrist do the extending instead of the fingers.
- *Progression:* Firmer resistance; single-finger work.
- *Regression:* Back to the light band.

**`finger-ext-eccentric`** — Open against the band, then take 4 slow seconds to close.
- *Cue:* Count "one-two-three-four" on the way closed, every rep.
- *Mistakes:* Snapping shut; skipping the count when you get tired.
- *Progression:* Firmer band; 6-second close.
- *Regression:* 2-second close.

**`putty-firm-grip`** — Green/blue putty through the full circuit (gross grip, pinch, spread).
- *Cue:* Same rules; the grade jump should feel hard but not painful in the joints.
- *Mistakes:* Jumping two grades; joint pain during (that's a stop signal, unlike muscular
  fatigue).
- *Progression:* Next grade.
- *Regression:* Back a grade.

**`gripper-moderate`** — Mid-setting gripper for 8–12 hard reps. Expect meaningful crush-grip
gains over 4–12 weeks [20].
- *Cue:* Set the handle deep in the palm before the first rep.
- *Mistakes:* Letting the gripper slide; reps that don't fully close.
- *Progression:* Harder setting → `gripper-heavy-holds`.
- *Regression:* Easier setting; drop to `gripper-light`.

**`rice-bucket-circuit`** — Hand buried in a bucket of rice: squeeze and release, open the hand
against the rice, rotate the forearm, dig, grab and pull out. Its real value is **extensor work
and support-grip endurance**, not maximal strength; the formal evidence base is thin and mostly
rehab-practice tradition [30].
- *Cue:* Bury to the wrist and open the hand *against* the rice — the extension is the point.
- *Mistakes:* Only doing flexion drills (that's just a squishy gripper); going so long the
  forearm cramps.
- *Progression:* Deeper bucket, longer intervals, add wrist rotation drills.
- *Regression:* Shorter intervals; use a bowl of dry beans.

**`pinch-plate-hold`** — Pinch two smooth plates together (smooth sides out) or a pinch block and
hold. Pinch is a separate grip quality from crush and has to be trained on its own [20][22].
- *Cue:* Thumb pad flat against the plate; keep the wrist straight.
- *Mistakes:* Letting the plates rest on the fingers instead of pinching; jerking on the pickup.
- *Progression:* Heavier plates; longer holds; `pinch-block-weighted`.
- *Regression:* One lighter plate; a thick book.

**`wall-fingertip-pushup`** — Push-up against a wall with only the fingertips (and thumb) in
contact. **Speculative for jam-proofing** — the rationale is loading the finger joints in
compression through a functional range, borrowed from climbing/gymnastics practice, not from a
prevention trial.
- *Cue:* Fingers slightly bent and "domed," never collapsed flat or hyperextended.
- *Mistakes:* Doing this on the floor first; letting the joints buckle backward.
- *Progression:* Step feet back; then incline; then `fingertip-pushup-floor`.
- *Regression:* Stand closer; use four fingers plus the palm heel.

**`dead-hang-support`** — Hang from a bar with an open support grip. Trains the support grip
quality (distinct from crush) and generally loads the wrist gently in traction [32].
- *Cue:* Shoulders slightly engaged, not fully passive; breathe.
- *Mistakes:* Doing them with an active wrist injury — hangs are contraindicated with acute
  wrist/hand injuries [32]. Dropping off the bar suddenly.
- *Progression:* Longer holds → towel hang → `weighted-dead-hang` → `edge-hang-20mm`.
- *Regression:* Feet on a box taking part of the weight.

**`towel-hang-or-carry`** — Hang from towels draped over a bar, or carry dumbbells wrapped in a
towel.
- *Cue:* Thick, unstable grip — that's the point.
- *Mistakes:* Grinding the palms raw; going to failure every set.
- *Progression:* Thicker towel; one towel per hand; add weight.
- *Regression:* Regular bar hang or standard farmer carry.

**`buddy-tape-catch-drill`** — Tape ring+middle (or the previously injured digit to its neighbor)
and run game-speed catching. This is the highest-confidence protective intervention in the whole
finger program: buddy taping mechanically blocks the sideways and hyperextension motion that
sprains a PIP joint, and it's the standard treatment for volar plate injuries [23][24][25][28].
- *Cue:* Tape **above and below the PIP joint**, never over it. Snug, not tourniquet-tight —
  you should still get quick capillary refill in the fingertip.
- *Mistakes:* Taping the index to the middle (kills ball control); taping across the joint
  itself; taping so tight the finger goes white or numb; using it as a substitute for technique.
- *Progression:* Wear it for competitive games; drop it for practice as tolerance builds.
- *Regression:* Wear it for everything, or use a pre-made buddy strap.

### Fingers — Level 3 (Advanced, optional)

**`gripper-heavy-holds`** — Close a hard gripper and hold the closed position 5–8 s.
- *Cue:* Set deep in the palm, close hard, then *keep* squeezing.
- *Mistakes:* Ego-loading a gripper you can't fully close; doing these while wrist symptoms
  linger.
- *Progression:* Harder gripper; longer holds.
- *Regression:* `gripper-moderate` reps.

**`pinch-block-weighted`** — Pinch grip on a block with weight hanging, held for time.
- *Cue:* Wrist straight, thumb pad flat.
- *Mistakes:* Loading fast; letting the block slip and catching it (a genuine finger injury risk).
- *Progression:* More weight; wider block.
- *Regression:* `pinch-plate-hold`.

**`edge-hang-20mm`** — Feet-assisted hangs on a **20 mm or larger** edge, open-hand or half-crimp,
7–10 s per set with full rest. The López protocol (3–5 × 10 s max hangs, 3 min rest, 2×/week,
keeping a ~3 s reserve) produced ~35% strength gains in lower-strength subjects and ~4% in
already-strong ones over 4 weeks — i.e. **big returns for beginners, near-nothing for the
already-strong** [19][21].
- *Cue:* Never go to failure — stop each hang with about 3 seconds still in the tank.
- *Mistakes:* Small edges (<20 mm), full crimp, bodyweight-plus loading, more than 2×/week. For
  a 49-year-old whose actual sport is basketball, small-edge crimping is all injury risk for no
  sport-specific return.
- *Progression:* More weight before smaller edges. Never the reverse.
- *Regression:* More foot assistance; `dead-hang-support`.
- *Note:* This is where the climbing-derived joint-thickening rationale [16][17] lives — and also
  where the osteoarthritis caveat [16] applies. Optional for a reason.

**`weighted-dead-hang`** — Support-grip hang with added weight.
- *Cue:* Add weight in small increments (5 lb).
- *Mistakes:* Jumping straight to a heavy vest.
- *Progression:* More weight, longer holds.
- *Regression:* Bodyweight hang.

**`fingertip-pushup-floor`** — Push-ups on fingertips from the knees, progressing to full.
**Speculative for jam-proofing.**
- *Cue:* Domed fingers, weight spread across all five digits, wrist straight.
- *Mistakes:* Full push-ups on day one; joints collapsing; ignoring finger joint aching.
- *Progression:* Knees → full → feet elevated.
- *Regression:* `wall-fingertip-pushup`.

**`reactive-catch-wall`** — Rapid-fire tennis ball and basketball catches off a wall, including
off-angle and one-handed.
- *Cue:* Track the ball all the way in; hands soft, ready, thumbs in on chest-height balls.
- *Mistakes:* Stabbing with straight fingers under time pressure — which is exactly the failure
  mode you're trying to eliminate, so slow down until the technique holds.
- *Progression:* Faster, closer, more unpredictable angles.
- *Regression:* Bigger, slower ball; two hands only.

**`weighted-ball-catch`** — Catch a 4–8 lb medicine ball from a partner or rebounder.
- *Cue:* Absorb — catch and give with the elbows over a good 6 inches of travel.
- *Mistakes:* Heavy ball with a hard catch; catching on the fingertips (that's exactly a jam).
- *Progression:* Heavier ball; more velocity.
- *Regression:* Lighter ball; catch off a bounce.

**`finger-maintenance-block`** — Band + putty + gripper, 2 sets each, twice a week, forever.

## 7. Interaction rules

The two programs share the same forearm, so they have to be sequenced, not just stacked.

**R1 — Wrist phase gates finger level.**

| Wrist status | Max finger level | Specifically banned |
|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 (acute/irritable) | **Base only** | Grippers above light, all hangs, rice bucket, pinch holds, fingertip push-ups |
| Phase 2 | Base, plus `gripper-light` and `rice-bucket-circuit` if painless | Weighted hangs, pinch blocks, `edge-hang-20mm`, floor fingertip push-ups |
| Phase 3 | Intermediate | `edge-hang-20mm`, `pinch-block-weighted`, `gripper-heavy-holds` |
| Phase 4 / discharged | Advanced (optional) | — |

**R2 — Extensor work is the exception and can start immediately.** `finger-ext-band` and the
putty spread work are low-load and pull in the *opposite* direction from the flexor-dominant
pattern that drives most overuse forearm pain [26]. They're safe in wrist Phase 1 and appear in
both programs.

**R3 — Grip work loads the wrist even when it doesn't feel like it.** Hard crushing recruits the
finger flexors that cross the wrist and co-contracts the wrist extensors to stabilize. If a
gripper session leaves the wrist sore the next morning, that's a wrist-program flare — apply the
24-hour rule and drop back a step in *both* programs.

**R4 — Hangs load the wrist in traction, not compression.** Push-ups compress and force wrist
extension; hangs distract and keep the wrist near neutral. So dead hangs usually clear *earlier*
than push-ups. Use `dead-hang-wrist-check` in Phase 3 as the test — if a 20-second passive hang is
painless with no 24-h flare, `dead-hang-support` and `towel-hang-or-carry` are green-lit even if
flat-palm push-ups aren't yet.

**R5 — Push-ups are the shared spine.** The same angle ladder serves both programs:

```
wall-lean-hold → wall-pushup → incline-pushup-high → incline-pushup-low
      → quadruped-wrist-rock → fist-pushup → full-pushup → plyo-pushup (optional)
                                     ↓ (fingers branch, once full-pushup is clean)
                       wall-fingertip-pushup → fingertip-pushup-floor
```
Never work fingertip variants at a rung the flat-palm version hasn't already cleared. The
gymnastics rehab model is explicit that weight-bearing tolerance is rebuilt by descending the
angle, not by pushing through [19][29].

**R6 — Don't progress both programs in the same week.** Add load to the wrist program *or* the
finger program, never both. If something flares, you want to know which one did it.

**R7 — Volume budget.** Total hard forearm/hand work: **3–4 sessions/week**, ideally not on
consecutive days, and count a pickleball session as one of them. Base-level couch work (band,
putty, tendon glides) is low enough load to run daily on top of that.

**R8 — Taping doesn't wait.** Buddy taping is protective equipment, not training. Use it in
games from day one regardless of what phase or level anything else is in [24][25].

**R9 — De Quervain variant blocks a specific thing.** If Finkelstein is positive, defer
`putty-tip-pinch` at firm grades, heavy `pinch-plate-hold`, and `radial-ulnar-hammer` (radial
half) until radial-side symptoms settle — thumb pinch loads the exact tendons involved [6].

**R10 — ECU variant blocks another.** If the ECU screen was positive, avoid loaded end-range
supination and any hanging/rotational drill that produces the snap, until it's been assessed [11].

## 8. Red flags — see a professional

**Wrist — stop the program and get assessed if:**

- **Focal deep ulnar-side (pinky-side) pain with forearm rotation and axial load**, especially
  with clicking, clunking, or a sense of instability → suspected **TFCC tear**. This is
  fibrocartilage and ligament, not tendon; untreated it can lead to persistent pain, DRUJ
  instability, and arthritis [12][13][14]. Progressive loading is the wrong tool.
- **A tendon that visibly snaps or pops over the bone** on the ulnar side (positive ice-cream
  scoop) → **ECU subsheath injury**, which frequently coexists with a TFCC tear [11][12].
- **Numbness, tingling, or pins-and-needles** anywhere in the hand → nerve involvement, not
  tendinopathy [12].
- Any **history of a fall onto an outstretched hand** with ongoing pain — especially anatomical
  snuffbox tenderness. Scaphoid fractures are notorious for being missed and for non-union [6].
- **Visible deformity, significant swelling, or hot/red skin.**
- **Night pain that wakes you**, or pain at complete rest, that isn't improving.
- **Locking, catching, or an inability to fully straighten a finger or the wrist.**
- **Grip strength that keeps declining** despite consistent loading.
- **No meaningful improvement after 8–12 weeks** of consistent, correctly-dosed loading. Most
  overuse tendinopathies improve within 3–6 months of a committed program [1][7]; a plateau at
  12 weeks means the diagnosis probably needs revisiting.
- **A note on injections:** corticosteroid injections are commonly offered and often help
  short-term, but for tendinopathy they're associated with **worse long-term outcomes** [2].
  Worth knowing before you're in the room.

**Fingers — do NOT rehab through these. Go get seen:**

- **Any visible deformity, angulation, or rotation** of the finger — including a fingertip that
  droops and won't straighten (**mallet finger**) or a middle joint that bends down while the
  tip cocks back (**boutonnière**). Both are extensor tendon injuries; both can become permanent
  deformities without prompt treatment; both get missed because "it's just a jammed finger"
  [26][27]. Don't wait to see if it improves.
- **Joint instability** — the finger feels loose, gives way sideways, or can be moved abnormally.
- **Inability to actively straighten or bend** the joint after a jam.
- **Severe swelling, obvious bruising, or a joint that won't move at all** after 2–3 days.
- **A jam that isn't clearly improving after 1–2 weeks**, or pain that's still increasing.
- **Any suspicion of a fracture** (point tenderness over bone, deformity, pain with axial load).
- **Numbness or a fingertip that's pale/cold** — including from taping too tight.

**Immediate management of a fresh jam** (while arranging assessment if any of the above apply):
ice, elevate, buddy-tape to an adjacent finger, and start **gentle** range of motion within a
few days rather than immobilizing for weeks — the standard course for a mild volar plate injury
is 1–2 weeks of protective buddy taping with early motion, not a splint left on until it feels
fine [23][24][25]. Prolonged immobilization is the main cause of the permanent stiffness people
end up with.
