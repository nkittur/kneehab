# Durability, Cardio & Weight Management — 49M Basketball + Pickleball

**Scope.** A training program to keep a 49-year-old recreational basketball and pickleball player on the court 3+ days/week through his 50s, with fat loss as a byproduct rather than the objective.

**Current status (as of writing).** Acute right anterior tibialis tendonitis, sports paused. Ongoing PFPS rehab on one knee. Right wrist tendonitis. Peloton currently aggravates the knee (assumed fixed via cleat float / fore-aft adjustment). This document describes the **full program that phases in as sport resumes**; Section 8 and the per-block notes flag what is safe to run *right now* during the pause.

**Not medical advice.** Everything here is an evidence-informed template, not a prescription. The acute tibialis anterior case and the PFPS knee should be running through an actual PT.

---

## 1. Summary

### The case for strength + tendon + balance + cardio as injury insurance at 49

The honest framing: at 49 you are not training to get better at basketball. You are training so that basketball doesn't take you out. Four levers, each with independent evidence:

**Neuromuscular warm-up programs actually prevent injuries — this is the strongest evidence in the document.** The FIFA 11+ (a ~20-minute structured warm-up of running drills, strength, plyometrics, and balance) reduces overall and lower-extremity injuries by roughly 30–46% in randomized trials, with meta-analytic knee-injury reduction of 46% (IRR 0.54) [7] and ankle-injury reduction of 33% (RR 0.67) [8][9]. That is a rate ratio from cluster-randomized trials in tens of thousands of athletes, not a mechanism story. Nothing else in this program has that quality of evidence behind it.

**Balance/proprioceptive training specifically prevents ankle sprains, and works best in people who have sprained before.** Pooled RCTs (7 trials, 3,726 participants) show RR 0.65 for ankle sprain incidence; in athletes with a prior sprain, a 36% risk reduction (RR 0.64), NNT ≈ 13 for recurrent sprains [11][12]. Primary prevention in the never-sprained is less clear. Given that ankle sprains are the single most common basketball injury and that falls/impacts with the floor drive nearly half of pickleball ED visits [31][32], this is the cheapest insurance available — much of it is doable standing in a kitchen.

**Eccentric hamstring work halves hamstring strain rate, with a caveat.** The headline meta-analysis (8,459 athletes) found IRR 0.49 — a 51% reduction — for programs including the Nordic hamstring exercise [10a]. A methodological reappraisal argues the effect is inconclusive once study selection and between-study heterogeneity are handled properly [10b]. Treat it as "probably helpful, cheap, low-risk," not as settled.

**Heavy strength and tendon-specific loading is what actually changes tissue capacity.** Tendons adapt to high-magnitude, slow, sustained load. Heavy Slow Resistance (HSR — a 6-second rep, ~3s up / ~3s down, 2–3x/week, progressing 15RM → 6RM over 12 weeks) produces outcomes equal to eccentric-only protocols for Achilles tendinopathy at 12 and 52 weeks, with markedly better adherence (92% vs 78%) [3], and comparable results for patellar tendinopathy in network meta-analysis [4]. HSR is not just rehab — it is the *maintenance dose* that keeps a 49-year-old's Achilles and patellar tendons tolerant of jumping.

**Strength at this age is a use-it-or-lose-it proposition, and power decays faster than strength.** The NSCA position statement on resistance training for older adults is explicit that moderate-to-heavy loading combined with power-oriented work (moderate load, fast concentric *intent*) is what preserves the qualities that matter for landing, decelerating, and not falling [13]. Master athletes who train retain type II fiber cross-sectional area and voluntary activation near young-adult values [13]. Two hard sessions a week is the floor, and it is enough.

**Cardio is the piece that pays off in two currencies.** Cardiorespiratory fitness has a dose-response relationship with all-cause mortality with no observed upper limit — elite fitness vs. low fitness was associated with ~80% lower mortality risk in 122,007 patients [30]. Separately, aerobic base determines whether you're gassed in the third game, and being gassed is when landing mechanics degrade. Zone 2 volume is the low-cost way to build it; one weekly high-intensity session is the high-yield add-on (4x4 protocols raise VO2max ~7% in 8 weeks, and were run safely twice weekly for five years by 70-year-olds in the Generation 100 trial) [16][16b].

### Realistic expectations

- **Injury prevention is risk *reduction*, not immunity.** A 40% reduction in injury rate still means injuries happen. The realistic goal is fewer of them, less severe, and faster return.
- **Compliance is the whole ballgame.** The FIFA 11+ trials that show effects are the ones where teams actually did it 2–3x/week. A program done 40% of the time delivers well under 40% of the benefit. This is why the context tags in Section 3 matter — a couch-tagged isometric you do daily beats a gym-tagged exercise you do monthly.
- **Tendons are slow.** Meaningful tendon adaptation runs on a 12-week clock minimum [3][4]; the acute tibialis anterior case is typically 4–8 weeks of load management before it stops being the limiting factor. Do not expect the PFPS knee or the wrist to be "fixed" inside a month.
- **Recovery windows have widened and you can't negotiate with that.** Masters athletes take longer to recover from high-intensity work than training-matched younger athletes [33][34]. Where a 30-year-old runs back-to-back hard days, plan 48–72h between hard sessions loading the same tissue. Most masters-athlete injuries are scheduling failures, not exercise-selection failures.
- **Weight loss from training alone is modest.** Exercise below ~150 min/week produces minimal weight change; 150 min/week gets you roughly 2–3 kg; 225–420 min/week is what's needed for 5–7.5 kg without dietary change [20][21]. Training's real role in body composition is preserving lean mass and making the fat you do lose come from fat (resistance training preserves ~93% of the lean mass otherwise lost during caloric restriction in older adults) [22][23]. See Section 6.
- **Some of this is contested.** Zone 2's specific claims about mitochondrial optimality are debated [14][39]; the Nordic effect size is disputed [10b]; acute:chronic workload ratio thresholds have been substantially criticized [35]. Where a claim is shakier, this document says so.

## 2. Weekly template

### Design constraints

1. Sport (basketball/pickleball) is the hard day. Everything else is scheduled around it.
2. Two heavy strength sessions per week, ≥48h apart [13][19].
3. Tendon isometrics are near-daily; tendon HSR is 2–3x/week on non-consecutive days [3][4].
4. Balance/landing work rides along as a pre-sport warm-up (this is how the FIFA 11+ was actually trialed) [7][9], plus one standalone block.
5. Zone 2 fills the remaining aerobic volume; 0–1 interval sessions per week.
6. Total moderate-or-harder aerobic minutes (sport counts) target: 150–300/week [19], drifting toward the top of that range for the weight-management goal [20].

### Baseline week — 3 sport days (Tue / Thu / Sat)

The rule "no heavy lower-body strength the day before sport" (R4) is what forces this layout. With sport on Tue/Thu/Sat, the only legal slots for heavy lower-body work are the sport days themselves (after play) or Wednesday/Friday — and Friday only if Saturday's session is being treated as lighter. The default below puts lifting on the sport days.

| Day | Primary | Secondary | Approx. minutes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mon** | Zone 2 (elliptical or Peloton) | Tendon isometrics, balance-while-TV | 30–60 |
| **Tue** | **SPORT** (preceded by 12-min court warm-up) | — | 60–120 |
| **Tue PM** (same day, after play) | **Strength A** — heavy lower + upper push | Tendon HSR (calf/patellar) | 35–50 |
| **Wed** | Zone 2 easy *or* rest | Tendon isometrics, balance-while-TV | 0–40 |
| **Thu** | **SPORT** (preceded by 12-min court warm-up) | — | 60–120 |
| **Thu PM** (same day, after play) | **Strength B** — hinge/posterior + upper pull + Copenhagen/Nordic | Tendon isometrics | 35–50 |
| **Fri** | Zone 2 + standalone balance block (balance items only — no plyos, R8) | Upper-body accessories if Strength B was cut short (R9) | 30–60 |
| **Sat** | **SPORT** (preceded by 12-min court warm-up) | — | 60–120 |
| **Sun** | Long Zone 2 *or* optional intervals *or* full rest | Tendon isometrics, mobility | 0–60 |

The preferred pattern is **strength stacked onto sport days** (lift a few hours after playing, or immediately after), which concentrates hard stress into fewer days and leaves genuinely easy days between. That is the masters-athlete-friendly arrangement: fewer, bigger hard days rather than a smear of medium days.

*If lifting after playing isn't realistic:* move sport to Mon/Wed/Sat, put Strength A on Tue and Strength B on Thu (each one day *after* sport, permitted by R6), and keep Friday clear of lower-body work ahead of Saturday. The constraint that never bends is R4 — whatever the layout, the day before a court session has no heavy lower-body strength and no plyometrics in it.

### Sequencing rules (explicit — these are the scheduling algorithm)

**Hard/easy classification**

- R1. Classify each planned item as HARD or EASY. HARD = sport session, heavy strength (lower or upper), plyometric/landing block, interval cardio. EASY = Zone 2, recovery walk, isometrics, band work, balance-on-floor, mobility.
- R2. Cap HARD days at **4 per week**. Absolute ceiling 5, and only in a week with no interval session and no plyometric block.
- R3. Never schedule 3 consecutive HARD days. Two consecutive is permitted only if the second is a lift and the first is sport (or vice versa) — never sport-on-sport-on-sport.

**Lower-body / sport interaction**

- R4. **No heavy lower-body strength on the calendar day before a sport day.** This is the highest-priority rule; if it conflicts with anything below, this wins.
- R5. Heavy lower-body strength on the **same day as sport** is allowed and preferred — schedule it *after* play, ideally ≥2h after, never before.
- R6. Heavy lower-body strength the **day after** sport is allowed if the sport day was ≤90 minutes and there was no notable soreness on waking.
- R7. Minimum **48h between heavy lower-body sessions**; 72h if the previous session included new exercises, added load, or produced next-day soreness ≥3/10 [33][34].
- R8. Plyometric/landing volume (`pogo-hops`, `snapdown-stick`, `lateral-bound-stick`, `single-leg-hop-hold`) counts as lower-body load. Never on the day before sport; never on the same day as heavy lower-body strength beyond the warm-up dose.

**Upper body / wrist**

- R9. Heavy upper-body strength has no restriction relative to sport days — it can go the day before basketball. It's the one lever that stays free.
- R10. While the right wrist is symptomatic: **no loaded wrist extension, no loaded gripping at end-range, no pushing on an extended wrist.** All pressing goes to neutral-grip dumbbells, parallettes, or landmine. Pulling uses neutral or (later) a strap. Wrist extensor isometrics (`wrist-extensor-isometric`) are daily and are *treatment*, not training load [25][26].
- R11. Pickleball loads the wrist and elbow directly. In any week with 2+ pickleball sessions, drop loaded carries (`suitcase-carry`) entirely.

**Tendon work**

- R12. **Tendon isometrics can be done daily**, including on sport days, including on rest days, including twice a day if symptomatic. They are analgesic and low-load [5][6][43]. They never count against the HARD-day budget.
- R13. **Tendon HSR (heavy, slow, 6-second reps) counts as strength load.** Calf/Achilles HSR goes into the strength sessions, 2–3x/week, non-consecutive days [3].
- R14. Isometrics 10–20 minutes *before* sport can be used deliberately for the PFPS knee and the patellar tendon — the analgesic window runs roughly 45 minutes [5]. Do not use this to mask a tendon that is actually flaring (Section 8).
- R15. Anterior tibialis work while acute: **isometrics only**, no resisted dorsiflexion through range, no downhill/decline walking, no barefoot sprinting starts. Progress to `tib-anterior-raise-band` only when isometrics are pain-free at full effort.

**Cardio**

- R16. **Zone 2 is unrestricted.** It can go on any day including the day before sport, including the same day as strength, including twice in a day. It is the volume filler.
- R17. **Maximum one interval session per week.** Zero in any week with 4 sport days.
- R18. Interval sessions must be ≥48h from a sport day on either side, and never within 24h of heavy lower-body strength. In practice this means: intervals only on Sunday in the baseline week, or dropped.
- R19. Prefer **non-impact modalities** for interval work (Peloton, elliptical) until the tibialis anterior is fully quiet and PFPS is stable through a full sport week. Court conditioning shuttles are the last thing added, not the first.
- R20. If Peloton reproduces knee pain after cleat/float/saddle adjustment, switch to elliptical and revisit the fit — do not "push through" a bike fit problem, which is a mechanical issue, not a fitness one.

**Week-shape adaptation**

- R21. **4 sport days in a week** → drop to **one** heavy strength session (make it the full-body Strength A), drop the interval session entirely, keep tendon isometrics daily, keep the court warm-up before every session, keep Zone 2 only on the true off day. Sport *is* the training that week.
- R22. **5+ sport days** → strength becomes a single ~20-minute maintenance dose (2 lower exercises, 2 upper, tendon HSR), no plyometrics outside warm-ups, no intervals. Expect this to be sustainable for ~2 weeks, not indefinitely.
- R23. **2 sport days** → add the second strength session back plus one interval session, and add one long Zone 2 (60 min). This is the week where fitness actually gets built.
- R24. **0 sport days (travel, illness, current pause)** → strength 2–3x, Zone 2 4–5x, full tendon protocol, balance daily. This is the current state and it is the best window available for tendon work.
- R25. Every **4th week is a deload**: cut strength volume ~40% (drop sets, keep load), no intervals, Zone 2 unchanged, sport unchanged. Non-negotiable at 49 [13][33].
- R26. Progression cap: increase weekly training *volume* (total minutes + total hard sets) by no more than roughly 10% week to week, and avoid re-entry spikes after any layoff. The specific acute:chronic ratio thresholds are contested and should not be treated as a precise dial [35], but "don't jump from 2 sport days to 5 in one week" is safe and uncontroversial advice.
- R27. **Return-from-pause ramp:** first week back is 1 sport day at ~50% duration; second week 2 sport days; third week 3. No plyometric block and no intervals until the third week.

## 3. Program blocks with exercise tables

**Context tags** — `couch` (seated, no sweat, doable watching TV) · `standing` (quick, anywhere) · `floor` (mat needed) · `gym` (equipment/space) · `sweat` (gets you sweaty).

Tags are honest: most of the strength work is `gym` or `floor`. The `couch`/`standing` subset in Blocks C and D is the genuinely useful low-friction material — that's the stuff that actually gets done daily.

### (a) Lower-body strength for court sports — PFPS-safe selection

PFPS principle: **combine hip-targeted and knee-targeted exercise**, and weight hip work more heavily early [1][2][36][37]. Manage patellofemoral joint stress by *knee flexion angle under load* rather than by avoiding loading altogether — closed-chain work is generally kept to 0–45/60° of knee flexion during symptom management, and open-chain knee extension to the 90–45° range, though the assumption that open-chain is inherently more stressful does not hold up in simulation [27]. Depth is the dial; load is the second dial.

| id | name | sets | reps / seconds | tempo | load / equipment | frequency | context |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `goblet-box-squat` | Goblet squat to box (depth-limited) | 3–4 | 6–10 | 3-1-1 | Dumbbell or kettlebell; box set so knee flexion ≈ 50–60° | 1–2x/wk | gym |
| `dumbbell-rdl` | Dumbbell Romanian deadlift | 3–4 | 6–10 | 3-1-1 | Dumbbells or kettlebells, neutral grip | 1–2x/wk | gym |
| `split-squat-short-stride` | Short-stride rear-foot-supported split squat | 3 | 8–10 / leg | 3-0-1 | Bodyweight → dumbbells; back foot on low step, not high bench | 1–2x/wk | gym |
| `step-down-eccentric` | Eccentric step-down | 3 | 6–10 / leg | 4-0-1 | 4–8" step, bodyweight → light dumbbells | 1–2x/wk | gym |
| `hip-thrust` | Hip thrust / bridge on bench | 3 | 8–12 | 2-2-1 | Barbell, dumbbell, or band; bench or couch edge | 2x/wk | gym |
| `nordic-hamstring-curl` | Nordic hamstring curl (assisted) | 2–3 | 4–6 | 4-0-X | Anchored feet + band assist or partner | 1–2x/wk | floor |
| `copenhagen-plank` | Copenhagen adductor plank | 2–3 | 15–30 s / side | isometric | Bench or couch for top-leg support | 2x/wk | floor |
| `band-lateral-walk` | Banded lateral walk (monster walk) | 2–3 | 12–15 steps / direction | 2-1-2 | Mini loop band above knees | 2–3x/wk | standing |
| `side-plank-hip-abduction` | Side plank with top-leg abduction | 2–3 | 8–12 / side | 2-2-2 | Bodyweight → ankle band | 2–3x/wk | floor |
| `sled-push-or-incline-carry` | Sled push / heavy incline treadmill push | 3–4 | 20–30 m or 30 s | continuous | Sled, or steep treadmill walk with weight vest | 1x/wk optional | sweat |

*PFPS substitutions:* if `goblet-box-squat` provokes pain, raise the box (shallower); if still symptomatic, replace with `spanish-squat-isometric` + `hip-thrust`. If `step-down-eccentric` is provocative, drop to a 2" step or replace with `split-squat-short-stride`. If `split-squat-short-stride` hurts at the front knee, shorten the stride further (more vertical shin) and reduce depth. Hip-dominant work (`hip-thrust`, `dumbbell-rdl`, `band-lateral-walk`, `side-plank-hip-abduction`) is the PFPS-safe backbone and should never need substitution [1][2][36][37].

*Anterior tibialis note:* while acute, avoid `sled-push-or-incline-carry` and any sustained decline walking. Everything else in this block is fine.

Defer `suitcase-carry` and any farmer's-carry/grip work until wrist symptoms are absent at rest and `wrist-extensor-isometric` is pain-free at full effort.

### (c) Tendon health micro-doses

Two distinct doses: **isometrics** (near-daily, analgesic, low-cost, do them anywhere) and **HSR** (2–3x/week, heavy, slow, 6-second reps, inside strength sessions). The HSR progression across a 12-week block: 3×15RM (wk 1) → 3×12RM (wk 2–3) → 4×10RM (wk 4–5) → 4×8RM (wk 6–8) → 4×6RM (wk 9–12) [3][4][40].

*Right now (sports paused):* `tib-anterior-isometric`, `wrist-extensor-isometric`, `seated-soleus-raise`, `knee-extension-isometric-band`, `ankle-eversion-band` are the daily protocol. All are couch-tagged. This is the most valuable thing available during the pause.

The pre-sport block is a compressed FIFA 11+: running/dynamic prep → strength/balance → plyometrics/landing → sport-specific movement [7][9]. Run it before **every** court session; that's the dosing at which it was trialed.

During the current pause, run only the balance items (`single-leg-balance-eyes-closed`, `single-leg-balance-perturbation`, `y-balance-reach`); they load the anterior tibialis minimally and are prime TV-time material.

### (e) Cardio menu

**Heart-rate anchors for a 49-year-old.** Estimated HRmax via Tanaka (208 − 0.7 × age) = **208 − 34.3 ≈ 174 bpm** [17]. The 220−age formula would give 171 and systematically underestimates in over-50s. Individual variation around any age-based estimate is roughly ±7–12 bpm, so **the talk test outranks the number**.

| Zone | % HRmax | bpm (HRmax 174) | RPE (0–10) | Talk test |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Recovery (Z1) | < 65% | < 113 | 2–3 | Conversation is effortless |
| **Zone 2** | ~70–80% | **~122–139** | 3–4 | Full sentences, slightly labored; nasal breathing still possible |
| Tempo/threshold | 82–88% | 143–153 | 6–7 | Short phrases only |
| **Interval (4x4)** | 90–95% | **157–165** | 8–9 | A few words at most |

Zone 2 is defined by *staying below the first ventilatory/lactate threshold* — the number is a proxy, the talk test is the referee. If you can't finish a sentence, you've drifted out of Zone 2, which is the single most common error. Expect HR drift upward of 5–10 bpm over a 60-minute session at constant power; hold the *effort*, not the number.

| `peloton-zone2` | Peloton steady Zone 2 | 1 | 30–60 min | continuous | Bike; moderate resistance, cadence 85–95; 122–139 bpm | 2–4x/wk | sweat |

| `elliptical-30-30` | 30/30 short intervals | 2–3 blocks | 8–10 × (30 s hard / 30 s easy) | — | Elliptical; hard = RPE 8, 3 min between blocks | 0–1x/wk (alternative to 4x4) | sweat |
| `bike-10x1` | 10 × 1-minute intervals | 1 | 10 × (1 min hard / 1 min easy) | — | Peloton; RPE 8–9 | 0–1x/wk (alternative to 4x4) | sweat |
| `court-conditioning-shuttles` | Suicides / lane shuttles | 6–10 | 15–25 s work / 45–60 s rest | max effort | Court; last modality added | 0–1x/wk, late phase | sweat |

*Modality rules:* while the anterior tibialis is symptomatic, **elliptical and bike only** — no incline treadmill (loaded dorsiflexion), no running, no shuttles. Once the bike fit is corrected, Peloton becomes the workhorse for both Zone 2 and intervals because it's zero-impact and the resistance is quantified. Add `incline-walk-zone2` when the tibialis is quiet, `court-conditioning-shuttles` only after 3–4 uninterrupted weeks of full sport.

*Volume target:* 150–300 min/week of moderate-or-harder aerobic activity, sport included [19]. For weight-management benefit, aim toward 250–300+ [20][21].

## 4. Session variants

### Strength day — SHORT (~15–20 min)

Two heavy compounds, one tendon dose, done.

1. `goblet-box-squat` — 3 × 8 (or `dumbbell-rdl` on Strength B day)
2. `chest-supported-row-neutral` — 3 × 10
3. `calf-raise-straight-knee` — 3 × 10 (HSR tempo)
4. `spanish-squat-isometric` — 3 × 45 s

Superset 1 and 2 to save time. Skip warm-up sets only if you've already played or done Zone 2.

### Strength day — MEDIUM (~30–45 min)

*Strength A (squat/push bias):*
1. Warm-up: `band-lateral-walk` 2 × 12, `pogo-hops` 2 × 15
2. `goblet-box-squat` — 4 × 6–8
3. `neutral-grip-db-floor-press` — 3 × 8 (superset with #2)
4. `split-squat-short-stride` — 3 × 8 / leg
5. `chest-supported-row-neutral` — 3 × 10 (superset with #4)
6. `calf-raise-straight-knee` — 4 × 8 (HSR)
7. `side-plank-hip-abduction` — 2 × 10 / side, `dead-bug` — 2 × 10 / side

*Strength B (hinge/pull bias):*
1. Warm-up: `band-pull-apart` 2 × 15, `single-leg-balance-perturbation` 2 × 30 s
2. `dumbbell-rdl` — 4 × 6–8
3. `trx-row-neutral` — 3 × 10 (superset)
4. `hip-thrust` — 3 × 10
5. `half-kneeling-landmine-press` — 3 × 8 / side (superset)
6. `nordic-hamstring-curl` — 3 × 5
7. `copenhagen-plank` — 3 × 20 s / side, `pallof-press` — 2 × 8 / side

### Strength day — LONG (~60 min)

Medium session, plus:
- Landing block up front (post-warm-up, pre-lifting): `snapdown-stick` 3 × 5, `lateral-bound-stick` 3 × 5 / side, `single-leg-hop-hold` 3 × 4 / side
- Add `step-down-eccentric` 3 × 8 / leg (Strength A) or `sled-push-or-incline-carry` 3 × 30 s (Strength B)
- Add `face-pull-band` 3 × 15 and `y-balance-reach` 2 × 5 / direction
- Finish with the full tendon dose: `calf-raise-straight-knee` 4 × 8, `seated-soleus-raise` 3 × 15, `knee-extension-isometric-band` 5 × 45 s

### Cardio day — SHORT (~15–20 min)

- Option A (base): `peloton-zone2` or `elliptical-zone2`, 18 min flat Zone 2. Twenty easy minutes still counts toward weekly volume; the 10-minute-bout minimum was removed from the guidelines [19].
- Option B (intensity, ≤1x/wk): `bike-10x1` — 4 min warm-up, 10 × (1 min @ RPE 8–9 / 1 min easy), 3 min cool-down.

### Cardio day — MEDIUM (~30–45 min)

- Option A (base): `elliptical-zone2` 40 min at 122–139 bpm. Watch something; this is the boredom-tolerant slot.
- Option B (intensity, ≤1x/wk): `peloton-4x4` — 10 min warm-up, 4 × (4 min @ 157–165 bpm / 3 min easy), 5 min cool-down ≈ 43 min total. This is the highest-yield VO2max session in the program [16][16b].

### Cardio day — LONG (~60 min)

- `peloton-zone2` or `elliptical-zone2`, 60 min continuous Zone 2, or 45 min Zone 2 + 15 min `recovery-walk`.
- Optional add-on for a mixed long day: 45 min Zone 2 followed by `elliptical-30-30` × 2 blocks — but only if this is the week's single intensity session (R17) and it isn't within 48h of sport (R18).
- Long Zone 2 is where the polarized-training logic pays: keep ~80% of weekly aerobic minutes genuinely easy and ~15–20% genuinely hard, with as little time as possible in the middle [14][15].

## 5. Exercise details

### Block A — Lower body

**`goblet-box-squat`** — Hold a dumbbell/kettlebell at the chest, sit back to a box set so the knee bends ~50–60°, pause, drive up. *Cue:* "Sit back to the box, chest tall — the box tells you where to stop." *Mistakes:* dropping onto the box instead of controlling down; letting knees cave in; going deeper than the box out of habit. *Progression:* heavier bell → lower box (only if pain-free) → barbell front squat. *Regression:* higher box, bodyweight only, or replace with `spanish-squat-isometric`.

**`dumbbell-rdl`** — Dumbbells at thighs, soft knees, push hips back with a flat back until you feel a hamstring stretch (~mid-shin), then drive hips forward. *Cue:* "Push your hips back to the wall behind you." *Mistakes:* squatting instead of hinging; rounding the low back; going deeper than hamstring flexibility allows. *Progression:* heavier, then single-leg RDL. *Regression:* shorter range, hands sliding down thighs, or band-resisted hip hinge.

**`split-squat-short-stride`** — Rear foot on a low step (4–8", not a bench), stride short enough that the front shin stays close to vertical, lower with control, drive through the front heel. *Cue:* "Front shin vertical, back knee straight down." *Mistakes:* stride too long (hip flexor stretch takes over) or too short (front knee travels far forward — bad for PFPS); torso collapsing forward. *Progression:* dumbbells → deeper range → higher rear foot. *Regression:* both feet on floor (static lunge), hand on a wall for balance, reduce depth.

**`step-down-eccentric`** — Stand on a step on one leg, slowly lower the opposite heel toward the floor, tap, return. *Cue:* "Lower like you're placing an egg on the floor — 4 seconds down." *Mistakes:* dropping fast; pushing off the down leg; letting the standing knee drift inward. *Progression:* taller step → hold dumbbells → slower tempo. *Regression:* 2" step, hold a rail, or switch to `split-squat-short-stride`. **PFPS gatekeeper:** the height at which this is pain-free is a good proxy for how much knee flexion under load you currently tolerate.

**`hip-thrust`** — Upper back on a bench/couch edge, feet flat, load across hips, drive hips to full extension, squeeze glutes, lower under control. *Cue:* "Ribs down, finish with the glutes, not the low back." *Mistakes:* hyperextending the lumbar spine at the top; heels too far forward (turns it into a hamstring exercise); short range. *Progression:* barbell with pad → single-leg. *Regression:* floor glute bridge, band across hips.

**`nordic-hamstring-curl`** — Kneel with ankles anchored, body straight from knees to head, lower forward as slowly as possible, catch with hands, push back up. *Cue:* "Resist all the way down — the goal is to fail slowly, not to fail late." *Mistakes:* hinging at the hips (breaking the straight line); dropping fast; doing too many too soon (this exercise produces serious DOMS). *Progression:* less band assist → longer eccentric → more reps. *Regression:* heavy band assist, hands on a raised surface, or substitute Swiss-ball leg curls. **Start with 2 × 4 and stay there for two weeks.**

**`copenhagen-plank`** — Side-lying, top leg's inner ankle or knee resting on a bench, lift the hips and bottom leg into a straight line, hold. *Cue:* "Squeeze the bench with the top leg." *Mistakes:* letting hips sag or rotate back; starting with the long-lever (ankle-supported) version. *Progression:* knee-supported → ankle-supported → add reps/dynamic version. *Regression:* bottom knee on the floor, shorter holds.

**`band-lateral-walk`** — Mini band above the knees, quarter-squat, step sideways keeping tension, don't let feet click together. *Cue:* "Push the band apart the whole way." *Mistakes:* standing too upright; letting the trailing foot drag in; band too light to matter. *Progression:* heavier band, band at ankles, more steps. *Regression:* lighter band, fewer steps.

**`side-plank-hip-abduction`** — Forearm side plank, lift the top leg toward the ceiling with the toe slightly turned down, lower with control. *Cue:* "Lead with the heel, not the toe." *Mistakes:* rotating the pelvis backward; hiking the hip. *Progression:* ankle band or ankle weight, longer holds. *Regression:* bottom knee down, or side-lying clamshell.

**`sled-push-or-incline-carry`** — Push a loaded sled 20–30 m at a hard but steady pace, or walk a steep treadmill at 3.0 mph with a weight vest. *Cue:* "Long, driving steps — lean into it from the ankle, not the waist." *Mistakes:* choppy short steps; loading so heavy that it becomes a grind. *Progression:* more load or distance. *Regression:* lighter, shorter. **Skip entirely while the anterior tibialis is symptomatic.**

### Block B — Upper body and trunk

**`neutral-grip-db-floor-press`** — Lying on the floor, dumbbells with palms facing each other, press up, lower until the triceps touch the floor. *Cue:* "Elbows at 45 degrees, wrists stacked over elbows." *Mistakes:* letting the wrist bend back (the exact thing you're avoiding); flaring elbows to 90°. *Progression:* heavier, then bench press with dumbbells (fuller range). *Regression:* lighter, one arm at a time. **Floor press deliberately limits range — good while shoulders and wrists are being protected.**

**`push-up-parallettes`** — Hands on parallettes or hex dumbbell handles so the wrist stays neutral, body in a straight line, lower chest to handle level. *Cue:* "Squeeze your glutes — one straight line from ears to heels." *Mistakes:* sagging hips; flaring elbows; letting the head lead. *Progression:* hands lower → feet elevated → add pauses. *Regression:* hands on a countertop or bench.

**`half-kneeling-landmine-press`** — Half-kneeling (inside knee up on the pressing side), barbell end at the shoulder, press up and forward along the bar's arc. *Cue:* "Ribs down, press *away*, not straight up." *Mistakes:* leaning back to press; letting the front knee drift; gripping the bar too tightly. *Progression:* more load, then standing. *Regression:* lighter bar, two hands.

**`chest-supported-row-neutral`** — Face-down on a 30–45° incline bench, dumbbells hanging with palms facing each other, row to the ribs, pause, lower. *Cue:* "Pull with the elbow, pause when the dumbbells reach your ribs." *Mistakes:* using body english (defeats the chest support); shrugging; short range. *Progression:* heavier, longer pause. *Regression:* lighter, higher bench angle.

**`trx-row-neutral`** — Handles in a neutral grip, body straight, heels planted, pull chest to hands. *Cue:* "Body stays a plank the entire rep." *Mistakes:* hips sagging; pulling with the arms only; gripping so hard the forearm lights up (wrist-relevant — loosen the grip, hook the fingers). *Progression:* walk feet forward (more horizontal), feet elevated. *Regression:* walk feet back (more upright).

**`band-pull-apart`** — Band at arm's length in front, pull apart to the chest, squeeze shoulder blades, return slowly. *Cue:* "Pull the band to your sternum, not your neck." *Mistakes:* shrugging; letting the band snap back. *Progression:* heavier band, pause at end range. *Regression:* lighter band, wider start grip.

**`face-pull-band`** — Band anchored at eye height, pull toward the face with elbows high, rotating hands so the thumbs finish pointing back. *Cue:* "Elbows high, thumbs back." *Mistakes:* turning it into a row; anchoring too low. *Progression:* heavier band, 2-second hold. *Regression:* lighter band, drop the external rotation.

**`pallof-press`** — Band anchored at chest height to the side, hands at the sternum, press straight out and resist the rotational pull, return. *Cue:* "Don't let the band twist you." *Mistakes:* leaning away from the anchor; rushing; standing too close (no tension). *Progression:* step further from the anchor, add a hold, half-kneeling or split stance. *Regression:* lighter band, closer to the anchor.

**`dead-bug`** — On your back, arms up, hips and knees at 90°, press low back into the floor, extend opposite arm and leg, return. *Cue:* "Low back glued to the floor the whole time." *Mistakes:* low back arching (the only failure mode that matters); moving too fast; holding breath. *Progression:* straighten the extending leg fully, add a light weight or band. *Regression:* extend only the leg, or only the arm; shorten the range.

**`side-plank-forearm`** — Forearm and feet on the floor, body straight, hips lifted. *Cue:* "Push the floor away with the forearm — long body." *Mistakes:* sagging hips; rotating forward. *Progression:* longer holds, top leg lifted, feet stacked instead of staggered. *Regression:* bottom knee down.

**`suitcase-carry`** — Heavy weight in one hand, walk tall without leaning. *Cue:* "Stand like the weight isn't there." *Mistakes:* leaning away from the weight; shrugging; hurrying. *Progression:* heavier, longer distance. *Regression:* lighter, shorter. **Deferred entirely while the wrist is symptomatic — this is a maximal grip task.**

### Block C — Tendon

**`calf-raise-straight-knee`** — Forefoot on a step edge, knee straight, rise to full plantarflexion over ~3 s, hold 1 s, lower over ~3 s to a full stretch. *Cue:* "Six seconds a rep — count it out loud." *Mistakes:* bouncing; cutting the top range; going too light (this is meant to be *heavy* — a 15RM load is not a bodyweight load). *Progression:* the HSR wave — 15RM → 12RM → 10RM → 8RM → 6RM over 12 weeks [3][4]; then single-leg with a loaded backpack. *Regression:* two legs, flat ground, bodyweight.

**`seated-soleus-raise`** — Seated with feet flat, weight resting across the knees, drive the heels up as high as possible, lower slowly. *Cue:* "Heels as high as they go — pause at the top." *Mistakes:* short range; too little weight (a couple of dumbbells or a loaded backpack across the knees). *Progression:* more weight, single leg, longer holds. *Regression:* bodyweight only. **The best couch exercise in the program** — the soleus takes enormous load in court sports and is nearly always undertrained.

**`heel-drop-eccentric`** — Rise on two legs, shift to one, lower slowly off a step edge below the level of the step. *Cue:* "Up with two, down with one, four seconds." *Mistakes:* using both legs to lower; not going below step level. *Progression:* add a backpack; add a bent-knee version to bias soleus. *Regression:* both legs lowering, flat ground.

**`spanish-squat-isometric`** — Heavy band around the backs of the knees, anchored to a rack or post behind you; sit back into ~60–70° of knee flexion with the shins vertical, hold. *Cue:* "Shins vertical, let the band hold you back — sit into it." *Mistakes:* band too light (no support, becomes a wall sit); leaning forward; letting knees travel forward. *Progression:* longer holds, then add a weight vest, then convert to slow reps of 8–15. *Regression:* `wall-sit-isometric`. **Evidence note:** the analgesic isometric protocol (5 × 45 s at ~70% effort) comes from Rio et al. 2015, a crossover trial in *six* volleyball players showing immediate pain reduction lasting ≥45 min [5]. Later work found responders and non-responders and no consistent superiority over isotonic loading [6][25][43]. Try it; if it helps, use it pre-sport; if it doesn't, drop it and lean on the HSR.

**`wall-sit-isometric`** — Back against a wall, thighs approaching parallel, hold. *Cue:* "Feet far enough out that the shins stay vertical." *Mistakes:* feet too close to the wall (knees forward, more patellofemoral load); sliding down over time. *Progression:* longer holds, single leg, add a weight on the lap. *Regression:* shallower angle, shorter holds. Note that a wall sit reaches only ~60% of maximal quadriceps contraction — it's a starting point, not the endpoint [42].

**`knee-extension-isometric-band`** — Seated in a chair, band around the ankle anchored behind, push into ~60° of knee flexion at roughly 70% of max effort, hold 45 s, rest 2 min, ×5. *Cue:* "Hard but sustainable — you should be able to hold all 45 seconds." *Mistakes:* going to 100% effort (unsustainable, provocative); holding breath; too little rest between reps. *Progression:* heavier band, longer holds, move to a leg-extension machine. *Regression:* lighter band, 30 s holds. **Use 10–20 min before sport for the analgesic window** [5].

**`tib-anterior-isometric`** — Seated, band looped around the forefoot and anchored ahead, pull the foot toward the shin against resistance and hold at a **pain-free** sub-maximal effort. *Cue:* "Firm, not maximal — this should be quiet, not sharp." *Mistakes:* pushing into pain; going through range while acute; letting the toes do the work instead of the ankle. *Progression:* raise effort level → hold longer → move to `tib-anterior-raise-band`. *Regression:* less band tension, shorter holds, or isometric against a hand.

**`tib-anterior-raise-band`** — Same setup, but move through range: dorsiflex over 3 s, lower over 3 s. *Cue:* "Pull the toes up, then fight the way down." *Mistakes:* letting the foot snap back down; adding this too early. *Progression:* heavier band, then standing toe raises against a wall, then heel walks. *Regression:* back to isometrics. **Gate:** do not start until `tib-anterior-isometric` is pain-free at full effort and there is no morning pain [24][38].

**`ankle-eversion-band`** — Seated, band around the forefoot, turn the sole outward against resistance (and inward on a second set). *Cue:* "Move the ankle, keep the knee still." *Mistakes:* rotating the whole leg from the hip. *Progression:* heavier band, slower tempo, more reps. *Regression:* lighter band. Peroneal strength is the direct counterweight to inversion ankle sprains.

**`wrist-extensor-isometric`** — Elbow at ~90°, forearm supported, wrist in ~20–30° extension, band looped around the hand (not gripped in the fist), hold 30–60 s against light resistance, ×3, up to three times a day. *Cue:* "Light — 1 to 3 kg feels like nothing, and that's correct." *Mistakes:* gripping the band in a fist (recruits the finger flexors and adds load you don't want); using too much resistance; doing eccentrics while reactive. *Progression:* longer holds → more resistance → slow isotonic wrist extension → grip work. *Regression:* isometric against the other hand with no band. **Protocol from the lateral-elbow-tendinopathy literature** — gentle pain-free isometrics daily are the appropriate opener for an irritable tendon; eccentrics tend to aggravate [25][26].

**`forearm-pronation-supination`** — Hold a light hammer or a dumbbell by one end, elbow at 90° tucked to the side, rotate the palm up then down slowly. *Cue:* "Rotate from the forearm, elbow pinned to your ribs." *Mistakes:* moving the shoulder; too much weight. *Progression:* slide the grip further from the weighted end. *Regression:* lighter, shorter lever. **Post-acute only** — relevant for pickleball, which loads forearm rotation hard.

### Block D — Balance and landing

**`court-dynamic-warmup`** — 8–12 min: 2–3 min easy jog; hip openers and closers; lateral shuffles both directions; walking lunges; 3–4 build-up strides to ~80%; 3–4 defensive slides / split-step drills; finish with 2 easy layup or dink sequences. *Cue:* "Warm up until you'd be happy to sprint, then start." *Mistakes:* static stretching instead of dynamic movement; skipping it when you're late (this is exactly the day it matters); doing it at half effort. *Progression:* add the strength/plyo elements of the 11+ (planks, Nordics, hops) on days you have the extra 5 minutes. *Regression:* 5-minute version — jog, shuffles, strides, split-steps. **This is the single best-evidenced item in the program; do it before every session** [7][8][9].

**`single-leg-balance-eyes-closed`** — Stand on one leg, barefoot, close your eyes, hold 30 s. *Cue:* "Let the toes and ankle do the correcting — small adjustments, not big saves." *Mistakes:* holding onto something (defeats the purpose); locking the knee; giving up when it gets wobbly (the wobble *is* the training). *Progression:* foam pad or pillow → head turns → `single-leg-balance-perturbation`. *Regression:* eyes open, fingertip on a wall. **Do it while brushing teeth and while watching TV — that's the entire dosing strategy** [11][12].

**`single-leg-balance-perturbation`** — Single-leg stand while tossing a ball against a wall, turning the head side to side, or reaching with the free foot. *Cue:* "Eyes off your feet — track the ball." *Mistakes:* stiffening up; staring at the floor. *Progression:* unstable surface, eyes closed with head turns, catch with one hand. *Regression:* two feet, or single leg with no perturbation.

**`y-balance-reach`** — Balance on one leg, reach the other foot as far as possible forward, then back-and-in, then back-and-out, tapping lightly and returning without touching down. *Cue:* "Tap, don't plant." *Mistakes:* putting weight on the reaching foot; letting the stance knee cave in. *Progression:* further reaches, hold a light weight at the chest. *Regression:* shorter reaches, fingertip support.

**`pogo-hops`** — Small, fast, two-foot hops with stiff ankles and minimal knee bend, staying on the balls of the feet. *Cue:* "Stiff springs — minimize time on the ground, not maximize height." *Mistakes:* squatting between hops; heels touching down; going for height. *Progression:* single-leg pogos, then forward/lateral travel. *Regression:* fewer reps, lower amplitude, or on grass. **Excellent Achilles and tibialis-anterior stiffness training — but reintroduce it only after the tibialis is quiet.**

**`snapdown-stick`** — From a tall standing position, drop rapidly into an athletic quarter-squat landing position and freeze for 3 seconds. *Cue:* "Land quiet, freeze like a photograph." *Mistakes:* landing loudly (means you're absorbing with joints, not muscles); knees caving in; not actually holding the freeze. *Progression:* small box drop → single-leg snapdown. *Regression:* slower, shallower, from a lower height.

**`lateral-bound-stick`** — Push off one leg sideways, land on the opposite leg, stick and hold 2–3 s before the next rep. *Cue:* "Land on a stable knee — if you wobble, you jumped too far." *Mistakes:* bounding further than you can control; knee valgus on landing; rushing reps. *Progression:* greater distance, then continuous bounds. *Regression:* smaller distance, land on two feet.

**`single-leg-hop-hold`** — Hop forward on one leg, land on the same leg, stick and hold 3 s. *Cue:* "Nose, knee, toe stacked on landing." *Mistakes:* letting the knee collapse inward; hopping too far too soon. *Progression:* longer hops, then hop-hop-stick sequences, then multidirectional. *Regression:* two-foot takeoff, single-leg landing; or shorter distance.

**`shuffle-decel-drill`** — Shuffle 5 m, decelerate to a complete stop in an athletic position, change direction, repeat for 10–15 s. *Cue:* "Win the stop, not the start." *Mistakes:* rounding the change of direction instead of actually stopping; crossing the feet. *Progression:* longer distances, reactive (partner or app cue) rather than pre-planned. *Regression:* slower speed, shorter distance, pre-planned only. **Deceleration is where court injuries happen — this is deliberately practicing the risky moment under controlled conditions** [28][29].

### Block E — Cardio

*Shared Zone 2 rule for the three steady-state items below:* hold 122–139 bpm at RPE 3–4, able to speak full sentences. *Cue:* "If you can't finish a sentence, ease off — the discipline is staying slow." *Common mistakes across all three:* drifting into the grey zone above threshold (the single most common Zone 2 error); chasing a wattage or speed target instead of a physiological one; treating a hard class or a hilly route as Zone 2. *General progression:* extend duration first (30 → 45 → 60 min), then increase power/incline at the same heart rate — rising output at constant HR *is* the adaptation. *General regression:* shorter, or `recovery-walk`.

**`elliptical-zone2`** — Steady elliptical at 122–139 bpm, no arm-flailing, consistent stride rate. *Cue:* "Smooth and boring — if you're working, slow down." *Mistakes:* leaning on the handles (drops the effort without dropping the number); ramping resistance to make it feel like a workout. *Progression:* longer, then higher resistance at the same HR. *Regression:* shorter, or `recovery-walk`. **Lowest-impact option and the default while the tibialis anterior is symptomatic.**

**`peloton-zone2`** — Steady ride, cadence 85–95, moderate resistance, 122–139 bpm. *Cue:* "Spin light, not grind heavy." *Mistakes:* low-cadence/high-resistance riding, which turns a Zone 2 ride into a knee-loading strength session — likely part of why the bike has been provocative; using a scripted class whose intervals dictate the effort instead of your HR. *Progression:* longer, then more output at the same HR. *Regression:* lower resistance, shorter. *Fit note:* cleat float, cleat rotation, and fore-aft saddle position are the standard levers for cycling-related anterior knee pain; higher cadence at lower resistance is the first change to make alongside the fit adjustment.

**`incline-walk-zone2`** — Treadmill at 8–12% incline, 3.0–3.5 mph, 122–139 bpm, hands off the rails. *Cue:* "Hands off the handles — if you need them, lower the incline." *Mistakes:* holding on (invalidates the effort); running instead of walking; using this while the tibialis anterior is symptomatic — sustained incline walking loads dorsiflexion continuously. *Progression:* steeper incline, then a weight vest. *Regression:* lower incline, slower, or switch to `elliptical-zone2`. **Add only once the tibialis is quiet.**

**`recovery-walk`** — Easy outdoor walk under 113 bpm. *Cue:* "Conversational, nasal breathing." *Mistakes:* turning it into a workout. *Progression:* longer, or add light hills. *Regression:* shorter.

**`peloton-4x4`** — 10 min warm-up; 4 × [4 min at 157–165 bpm (RPE 8–9) / 3 min easy spinning]; 5 min cool-down. *Cue:* "Effort should be such that the fourth interval is barely completable — no more." *Mistakes:* starting interval 1 too hard and fading; cutting recovery short; doing this more than once a week at 49. *Progression:* raise the power held at the same heart rate; add a fifth interval only if the fourth still feels controlled. *Regression:* 3 intervals, or 3-minute work periods. **VO2max gains of ~7% in 8 weeks at 3x/week in middle-aged adults; run safely twice weekly for five years by 70-year-olds in Generation 100** [16][16b]. One per week is the right dose here given 3+ sport days.

**`elliptical-30-30`** — 2–3 blocks of 8–10 × (30 s at RPE 8 / 30 s easy), 3 min between blocks. *Cue:* "Repeatable — every hard 30 should look like the first." *Mistakes:* going all-out on the first block. *Progression:* more reps per block, then a third block. *Regression:* 20 s work / 40 s rest, fewer reps. A gentler alternative to 4x4 that accumulates similar time near VO2max with lower perceived misery.

**`bike-10x1`** — 10 × (1 min hard / 1 min easy). *Cue:* "Hard means you're counting down the seconds, not surviving." *Mistakes:* pacing the first three as sprints. *Progression:* 12 reps, or raise the target power. *Regression:* 6–8 reps. The best short-session intensity option (~24 min total).

**`court-conditioning-shuttles`** — 6–10 × 15–25 s max-effort shuttles with 45–60 s rest. *Cue:* "Touch the line — the change of direction is the point." *Mistakes:* adding these before the base is built; doing them the day before a game. *Progression:* more reps, shorter rest. *Regression:* fewer reps, longer rest, or replace with `bike-10x1`. **Last modality added; skip in any week with 3+ sport days — you're already getting this on the court.**

## 6. Weight management

Light-touch by design. No calorie tracking, no diet program.

**What training actually does.** Aerobic exercise alone produces modest weight loss, and the dose-response is roughly linear up to ~300 min/week: below 150 min/week, minimal change; at 150 min/week, roughly 2–3 kg; 225–420 min/week is what's required for 5–7.5 kg in the absence of dietary change [20][21]. Waist circumference and body-fat measures respond more reliably than scale weight [20]. So: **Zone 2 volume is the lever**, because it's the only modality you can accumulate 200–300 minutes a week of without wrecking recovery. Intervals are for VO2max, not for the scale; strength is for lean mass, not for the scale.

**Why the scale is the wrong instrument here.** Resistance training during a caloric deficit preserves the great majority of the lean mass otherwise lost — roughly 93% of caloric-restriction-induced lean-mass loss was prevented in older adults [23], and resistance training reliably reduces regional adiposity with larger effects in middle-aged and older adults [22]. If you're lifting twice a week and losing fat, the scale will move slower than the mirror and the belt. Track waist circumference monthly and how you feel in the fourth quarter; ignore day-to-day weight.

**Protein.** Total daily protein around **1.6 g/kg body weight** is the point past which additional intake stops adding to training-induced lean mass gains, with the confidence interval extending to ~2.2 g/kg [18]. The protein effect is *smaller* with increasing age, which is an argument for landing at the higher end rather than the lower. Practically: 1.6 g/kg for an 85 kg person is ~135 g/day, which is roughly 35–40 g at each of three meals plus a snack. Spread it out, get some after lifting, and don't overthink it. No supplement is required; whey or a shake is just a convenience for hitting the number.

**The rest of the diet, in one paragraph.** Fiber and protein at every meal make the volume of food self-limiting. Liquid calories are the highest-leverage single thing to cut, because they don't reduce hunger. Alcohol impairs recovery and sleep quality at exactly the age where both are already narrowing. That's it — there is no diet program here on purpose, because the training program is the thing that has to survive, and a simultaneous restrictive diet is the most reliable way to blow up recovery, which is how masters athletes get injured.

**One warning specific to this case.** Under-fueling around the sport days will not just slow recovery; it degrades late-game movement quality, and degraded late-game movement quality is when knees and ankles get hurt. Do not diet aggressively during a week with 4 sport days.

## 8. Red flags and deload rules

### Stop and see a clinician (not a deload — an evaluation)

- Sharp, localized pain that changes gait or shooting mechanics, especially if it appeared suddenly during a single movement.
- Any pop/snap at the moment of injury, with swelling.
- Knee swelling that appears within hours of activity, or a knee that gives way or locks.
- Anterior tibialis pain accompanied by numbness, foot drop, or weakness lifting the foot.
- Calf pain that is unilateral, warm, and present at rest (rule out DVT before assuming tendon).
- Wrist pain with night pain, numbness, or grip weakness that's worsening despite load reduction.
- Chest pressure, unusual shortness of breath, or lightheadedness during interval work. At 49, do not rationalize this.

### Tendon load monitoring — the operating rule

Use the Silbernagel pain-monitoring model, which is the evidence-based way to keep training *through* tendon rehab rather than stopping: **pain up to about 5/10 during and after loading is acceptable, provided it settles back to baseline by the next morning and does not increase week to week** [24]. Concretely:

- Pain during exercise ≤5/10 → proceed.
- Pain that has returned to baseline by the next morning → the load was appropriate; you may progress.
- Pain still elevated the next morning, or up more than ~2 points → the load was too much; repeat the previous week's load rather than progressing.
- Pain trending upward across weeks → back off a stage regardless of how any single session felt.

The **morning check** is the most reliable single signal for all three current problems: how the tibialis anterior, the PFPS knee, and the wrist feel in the first 20 minutes after waking is a better indicator than how they felt during yesterday's session.

### Signs of overreaching at 49 (as opposed to normal training fatigue)

- Resting heart rate elevated ≥5–7 bpm above your normal morning baseline for 3+ consecutive days.
- HRV trending down over a week (if you track it — treat as one input among several, not a verdict).
- Sleep disruption, particularly early waking, on a normal schedule.
- Multiple small niggles appearing simultaneously in different tissues. This is the classic masters-athlete pattern and it means systemic recovery capacity is exceeded, not that you have four separate injuries.
- Warm-up feeling worse than usual and *not* improving 15 minutes in. Normal stiffness improves with the warm-up; overreaching doesn't.
- Motivation drop specifically toward training you normally like.
- Losing a step in the third quarter you had two weeks ago.
- Persistent soreness lasting >72h after a session that used to take 48h.

### Deload rules

- **Scheduled:** every 4th week, cut strength volume ~40% (drop sets, keep loads), remove intervals, keep Zone 2 and sport unchanged [13].
- **Reactive (any 2 of the overreaching signs above):** take a full week at deload volume, drop to 2 sport days, no intervals, no plyometrics, keep tendon isometrics and Zone 2. Re-assess.
- **After a sport-heavy week (4+ sessions):** the following week is automatically a deload for strength — one maintenance session, no plyometrics (R21).
- **After any layoff >10 days:** re-enter at ~60% of previous volume and rebuild over 2–3 weeks. Injuries cluster at re-entry, not at peak [35].
- **Age-adjusted recovery floor:** 48–72h between hard sessions loading the same tissue; up to 72h+ after a novel or heavy lower-body session [33][34]. If you're guessing, take the longer number — at 49 the cost of one under-recovered session is higher than the cost of one missed session.

### Sport-specific risk notes

- Pickleball's injury profile in older players is dominated by **falls** and **sprains/strains** — roughly half of injuries involve impact with the floor, and falls are markedly more likely with age [31][32]. Balance and landing work is the direct countermeasure, and lunging for a dink is a common Achilles mechanism.
- Basketball's dominant injury is the ankle sprain, which is exactly what proprioceptive training reduces, with the largest effect in people who have sprained before [11][12].
- Both sports load the anterior tibialis heavily through rapid deceleration and the toes-up phase of shuffling and backpedalling. Given the current acute tendonitis, **backpedalling and hard lateral shuffling are the last movements to reintroduce**, not the first.
